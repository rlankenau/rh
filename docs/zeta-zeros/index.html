The zeros and the Riemann Hypothesis

A zero of ζ\zetaζ is a sss with ζ(s)=0\zeta(s) = 0ζ(s)=0. This chapter shows what looks like, where zeros can and cannot be, and states the .

What a zero is

ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is a complex number, with a and an imaginary part. It is 0 only when both parts are 0 at the same sss. Fix the real part σ\sigmaσ of sss and let the imaginary part ttt vary: the real part of ζ\zetaζ crosses 0 at some values of ttt, and the imaginary part of ζ\zetaζ crosses 0 at others. A zero of ζ\zetaζ needs both crossings to coincide.

The ∣ζ∣|\zeta|∣ζ∣ combines the two parts into one number, the distance of ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) from 0. A zero is where it touches 0.

A third view: plot the point ζ(σ+it)\zeta(\sigma + it)ζ(σ+it) in the and let ttt increase. The point traces a path that loops around. A zero is a moment when the path passes exactly through the origin.

The Riemann Hypothesis

The Riemann Hypothesis: every zero in the critical strip has real part exactly 12\tfrac1221​. Because zeros off the line come in mirror pairs, this is the same as saying that no zero has real part greater than 12\tfrac1221​.

Riemann computed the first few zeros by hand, found them on the line, and wrote that it is "very likely" that all are. What is known today:

  • There are infinitely many zeros in the strip, and infinitely many of them are on the line (Hardy, 1914). More than 40% of all zeros are known to be on the line.
  • There are no zeros on the edge σ=1\sigma = 1σ=1 (Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896). This fact is equivalent to the .
  • The first ten trillion zeros have been computed, and all are on the line.
  • No region of the form σ>c\sigma \gt cσ>c with c<1c \lt 1c<1 has been proved free of zeros.

How many zeros there are
