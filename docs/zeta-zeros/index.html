Part I · Chapter 5

The zeros and the Riemann Hypothesis

A zero of ζ is a s with ζ(s) = 0. This chapter shows what looks like, where zeros can and cannot be, and states the .

What a zero is

ζ(s) is a complex number, with a and an imaginary part. It is 0 only when both parts are 0 at the same s. Fix the real part σ of s and let the imaginary part t vary: the real part of ζ crosses 0 at some values of t, and the imaginary part of ζ crosses 0 at others. A zero of ζ needs both crossings to coincide.

The |ζ| combines the two parts into one number, the distance of ζ(s) from 0. A zero is where it touches 0.

A third view: plot the point ζ(σ + it) and let t increase. The point traces a path that loops around. A zero is a moment when the path passes exactly through the origin.

Where zeros can be

Three facts, all proved, limit where ζ(s) = 0 is possible.

  • None with σ > 1. There the product over primes converges, and none of its factors is 0 (chapter 3).
  • The . proved a formula connecting the values at s and at 1 − s: ζ(s) = 2s πs − 1 sin(πs/2) Γ(1 − s) ζ(1 − s). Here Γ is the gamma function, which extends the factorial. The point s = σ + it and the point 1 − s are mirror images through the point ½, so the formula determines ζ to the left of the line σ = ½ from its values to the right.
  • The . The factor sin(πs/2) is 0 at s = −2, −4, −6, …, and ζ is 0 there. These are called the trivial zeros. They are fully understood.

Together: for σ < 0 the functional equation expresses ζ(s) through ζ(1 − s), where 1 − s has real part greater than 1 and ζ is not 0. So the only zeros with σ < 0 are the trivial ones. Every other zero lies in the band 0 ≤ σ ≤ 1, called the . The line σ = ½ down its middle is the .

Within the strip the zeros have two symmetries. If ρ is a zero, so is its complex conjugate (same real part, opposite height), because ζ takes real values on the real axis. And so is 1 − ρ, by the functional equation. So a zero at β + iγ that is not on the critical line comes with a partner at (1 − β) + iγ on the other side of it.

The Riemann Hypothesis

The Riemann Hypothesis: every zero in the critical strip has real part exactly ½. Because zeros off the line come in mirror pairs, this is the same as saying that no zero has real part greater than ½.

Riemann computed the first few zeros by hand, found them on the line, and wrote that it is "very likely" that all are. What is known today:

  • There are infinitely many zeros in the strip, and infinitely many of them are on the line (Hardy, 1914). More than 40% of all zeros are known to be on the line.
  • There are no zeros on the edge σ = 1 (Hadamard and de la Vallée Poussin, 1896). This fact is equivalent to the .
  • The first ten trillion zeros have been computed, and all are on the line.
  • No region of the form σ > c with c < 1 has been proved free of zeros.

How many zeros there are

The zeros become denser with height. Riemann stated, and proved, a formula for the number N(T) of zeros in the strip with height between 0 and T:

N(T) ≈ (T/2π) · (T/2πe) + 7/8, with an error that grows only like ln T.

Why zeros matter for primes

Chapter 2 ended with −ζ′(s)/ζ(s) = (n)/ns, the function that carries the prime weights. It has ζ in the denominator. Wherever ζ(s) = 0, this function is infinite. So the zeros of ζ are the singular points of the function that encodes the primes.

How the labs compute ζ

The defining sum does not converge inside the critical strip, and the formula of chapter 4 converges slowly. The labs use a method due to , with 70 terms, which is a rapidly converging rearrangement of the sum. Checked against published values it is accurate to about six decimal places for heights up to 60, which is why the labs stop there.

Exercises

  1. Suppose ζ had a zero at 0.3 + 14i. Which other zeros would it force?
    Solution

    The complex conjugate, 0.3 − 14i. The functional equation pairs s with 1 − s, giving 0.7 − 14i, and its conjugate 0.7 + 14i. So one zero off the line means four, two of them to the right of it.

  2. Use the functional equation to explain why ζ(−2) = 0.
    Solution

    At s = −2 the factor sin(πs/2) = sin(−π) = 0. The other factors are finite: 2−2, π−3, Γ(3) = 2 and ζ(3) ≈ 1.202. So the product is 0.

  3. Use the counting formula to estimate how many zeros have height between 0 and 1,000.
    Solution

    (1000/2π) · ln(1000/(2πe)) + 7/8 ≈ 159.15 · ln 58.55 + 0.875 ≈ 159.15 · 4.070 + 0.875 ≈ 648.6. The true count is 649.

Check your understanding

  1. In Lab 5.1 at σ = 0.6, the real part of ζ still crosses 0 many times. Why are those not zeros of ζ?

    • Because 0.6 is not a whole number
    • A zero needs the real and the imaginary part to be 0 at the same point, and there the imaginary part is not 0
    • They are zeros

    One real equation in one unknown usually has solutions. A zero of ζ is two equations at once. That they are satisfied together at all, and apparently only on one line, is what makes the zeros remarkable.

  2. Why can there be no zeros with real part greater than 1, and none with real part less than 0 except the trivial ones?

    • Nobody has found any
    • Because the sum diverges there
    • For σ > 1 the product over primes has no zero factor; for σ < 0 the functional equation reduces ζ(s) to ζ(1 − s), which is in the first region

    Both are theorems. What remains is the strip 0 ≤ σ ≤ 1, where neither argument applies.

  3. In Lab 5.2 every dip lifts off the axis as σ moves from ½ to 0.6. If the Riemann Hypothesis were false, what would be seen somewhere far up the axis?

    • A dip that touches zero at some σ > ½, and not at ½
    • A dip that touches zero at every σ
    • The curve becoming negative

    A zero at β + it off the line means |ζ| = 0 on the vertical line σ = β, and by the mirror symmetry on σ = 1 − β, but not at ½. An absolute value is never negative.
