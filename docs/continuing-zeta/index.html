Continuing zeta

The sum for ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) has a value only when the of sss is greater than 1. This chapter extends ζ\zetaζ to every except s=1s = 1s=1: first to the half-plane σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0 in seven small steps, then to the rest.

Chapter 6 extended 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) beyond the interval where its series converges. The same thing can be done for ζ\zetaζ. The steps start with real sss.

From σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1 to σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0, in seven steps

Step 3. Notice that the bracket makes sense for every s≠1s \ne 1s=1

Look at the two pieces inside the bracket for a fixed NNN.

  • 1+12s+⋯+1Ns1 + \dfrac{1}{2^{s}} + \dots + \dfrac{1}{N^{s}}1+2s1​+⋯+Ns1​ is a sum of NNN numbers. It has a value for every sss.
  • N1−s1−s\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}1−sN1−s​ has a value for every sss except s=1s = 1s=1.

So the bracket can be computed for any s≠1s \ne 1s=1 and any NNN, including values of sss where the infinite sum diverges. The only question is whether it settles down as NNN grows.

Step 5. Why the difference settles

The subtracted term is an area under the curve: N1−s1−s=11−s+∫1Ndxxs\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s} = \dfrac{1}{1-s} + \displaystyle\int_{1}^{N}\frac{dx}{x^{s}}1−sN1−s​=1−s1​+∫1N​xsdx​. Split that area into the pieces over [1,2],[2,3],…[1,2], [2,3], \dots[1,2],[2,3],… and pair each piece with one term of the sum:

Each term in the new sum is the difference between a rectangle and the area under the curve above it, as in the picture of Lab 2.1. The curve gets flatter as nnn grows, so that difference is small: about s2 ns+1\dfrac{s}{2\,n^{s+1}}2ns+1s​ in size. The numbers 1/ns+11/n^{s+1}1/ns+1 have a finite sum whenever s>0s \gt 0s>0. So the bracket converges for every s>0s \gt 0s>0 except s=1s = 1s=1.

Step 6. The same formula for complex sss

Nothing in steps 2 to 5 needs sss to be real. For complex s=σ+its = \sigma + its=σ+it the bracket converges whenever σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0 and s≠1s \ne 1s=1.

In the picture of chapter 3: for 0<σ<10 \lt \sigma \lt 10<σ<1 the chain of vectors does not settle. It circles in an ever wider spiral, and the term N1−s/(1−s)N^{1-s}/(1-s)N1−s/(1−s) is exactly the vector from the center of that spiral to the chain's current end. Subtracting it leaves the center, and the center is ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s).

Step 7. Why this is the continuation

  • The limit of the bracket is a function defined for every sss with σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0 except s=1s = 1s=1.
  • For σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1 it equals the original sum (step 2).
  • It is analytic. (This needs a proof, which uses the fact that the convergence in step 5 is uniform.)

By the rigidity of chapter 5, there is only one analytic function with these properties. So the limit of the bracket is the continuation of ζ\zetaζ to the half-plane σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0.

From σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0 to the whole plane

So far ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is defined for every sss with real part σ>0\sigma \gt 0σ>0, except s=1s = 1s=1. This section extends it to the remaining half of the plane, in two ways, and then looks at two famous values.

First way: subtract more of what grows

In step 5 the bracket converged because subtracting N1−s1−s\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}1−sN1−s​ removed the part of the sum that grows fastest. For σ≤0\sigma \le 0σ≤0 that is no longer enough: a smaller growing part is left. It can be removed too. The pattern continues, and the first three corrections are:

ζ(s)  =  lim⁡N→∞[  ∑n=1N1ns  −  N1−s1−s⏟enough for σ>0  −  12⋅1Ns⏟enough for σ>−1  +  s12⋅1Ns+1⏟enough for σ>−3  ]\zeta(s) \;=\; \lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\;\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n^{s}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt 0} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{s}}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt -1} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{s}{12}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{s+1}}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt -3}\;\right]ζ(s)=N→∞lim​​n=1∑N​ns1​−enough for σ>01−sN1−s​​​−enough for σ>−121​⋅Ns1​​​+enough for σ>−312s​⋅Ns+11​​​​

The second correction is half of the last term. The third comes from the next term of the same expansion (the –Maclaurin formula, which refines the comparison between a sum and an ). Each further correction moves the boundary further left, and with enough of them any sss is reached. By rigidity, every one of these formulas gives the same function wherever two of them both apply.

  • The first correction is N11=N\dfrac{N^{1}}{1} = N1N1​=N. Subtracting it leaves N−N=0N - N = 0N−N=0.
  • The second correction is half of the last term, 12\tfrac1221​. Subtracting it leaves −12-\tfrac12−21​.
  • The third correction is 012⋅1N=0\dfrac{0}{12}\cdot\dfrac1N = 0120​⋅N1​=0.

The bracket equals −12-\tfrac12−21​ for every NNN. So ζ(0)=−12\zeta(0) = -\tfrac12ζ(0)=−21​.

Second way: a chain of discs around the pole

The same extension can be reached with Taylor series alone, as for 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) in Lab 6.2. The only bad point of ζ\zetaζ is s=1s = 1s=1. So by the rule of chapter 5, the Taylor series of ζ\zetaζ about any center ccc converges in the disc around ccc whose edge passes through 1.

Start at a center where the original sum converges, for example c=2c = 2c=2. Its disc reaches from 1 to 3. Pick a new center inside that disc, a little above the real axis. The function and all its derivatives are known there, so the Taylor series about the new center is known, and its disc covers points the first disc did not. Repeat. The chain of centers passes over the bad point and comes down on the other side.

did not use either of these step-by-step methods. He found formulas for ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) that are valid for every s≠1s \ne 1s=1 at once. One of them, the , is in chapter 8. By rigidity all of these routes give the same function.
