Part II · Chapter 8

Summing over whole numbers

Chapter 7 summed a function over the powers of γ, and the factor 1 − γ−s appeared in front of its . Summing over the whole numbers instead puts ζ(s) in front. The resulting functions are the of the paper.

Based on §1.1.4 of the paper (page 12), read in full. The lab works over the ordinary integers with the trivial character, which is the case; the paper works over any .

θ-functions

The paper uses the construction of chapter 7 with a different sum, the one at the centre of . Take a function f on (0, ∞) and sum its values at all whole-number multiples of x:

Θf(x) = √x · n ≥ 1 f(nx). The paper calls functions of this form θ-functions. The transform is M(Θf)(s) = ζ(s + ½) · M(f)(s + ½).

Proof: by the rescaling property the transform of f(nx) is n−s · M(f)(s), and Σ n−s = ζ(s). Multiplying by √x replaces s by s + ½. (The sum converges for Re s > ½; the identity extends to all s by when Θf decays at both ends.) The factor ζ here plays the role that 1 − γ−s played on the circle.

So the transform of every θ-function is zero at the zeros of ζ, whatever f is. Because of the shift by ½, ρ of ζ appears at s = ρ − ½. If the holds these points are all on the imaginary axis, at s = ±14.13i, ±21.02i, ±25.01i, …

Things to try

  • In Lab 8.1, move f around and change its width. The blue transform changes shape completely; the orange one keeps its zeros at the same heights.
  • Look for a setting where the blue curve itself dips close to zero between two dots. The orange curve dips there as well, but the ratio in the bottom panel does not: it depends only on ζ. (Very close to such a dip the ratio is a of two tiny numbers and gets noisy.)

Exercises

  1. Show that the Mellin transform of f(nx) is n−s · M(f)(s).
    Solution

    Substitute u = nx: ∫ f(nx) xs dx/x = ∫ f(u) (u/n)s du/u = n−s · M(f)(s). The measure dx/x is unchanged by rescaling.

  2. The first zero of ζ is ρ ≈ ½ + 14.13i. Where is the transform of Θf zero because of it?
    Solution

    At s = ρ − ½ ≈ 14.13i, since M(Θf)(s) contains the factor ζ(s + ½). That is the first dot in the lab.

  3. Why does Θf(x) blow up as x → 0 unless ∫ f dx = 0?
    Solution

    For small x the points nx are closely spaced, and x · Σ f(nx) is a sum for ∫ f. So Σ f(nx) ≈ (1/x) ∫ f, and Θf(x) ≈ x−½ ∫ f, which is unbounded unless the integral is 0.

Check your understanding

  1. The transform of Θf vanishes at s = ρ − ½ for every zero ρ, whichever f you choose. What does that tell you?

    • That f was chosen specially
    • The zeros are a property of summing over the whole numbers, not of f
    • That Θf is identically 0

    M(Θf)(s) = ζ(s + ½) · M(f)(s + ½). The factor ζ comes from Σ n−s and is the same for every f.

  2. What is the effect of the factor √x in the definition of Θf?

    • It shifts s by ½, so zeros on the critical line appear at purely imaginary s
    • It makes the sum converge
    • It removes the zeros

    Multiplying by x½ shifts the Mellin variable by ½. The critical line Re s = ½ becomes the imaginary axis, where the transform is an ordinary Fourier transform in ln x.

  3. Suppose you summed f(nx) over the odd n only. Which factor would appear in front of M(f)?

    • ζ(s) + 2−s
    • ζ(2s)
    • ζ(s) · (1 − 2−s)

    The even n contribute Σ (2m)−s = 2−sζ(s). Removing them leaves ζ(s)(1 − 2−s): the Euler product without the factor for the prime 2.
