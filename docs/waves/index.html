Zeros as waves

The writes ψ(x) as x minus one oscillating term for each zero of ζ. A zero with β contributes a term of size about xβ, so the real parts of the zeros determine the size of ψ(x) − x.

The explicit formula

wrote down, and later proved, an exact equation between the ψ(x) of chapter 1 and the in the . It is called the explicit formula. Each zero is a , written ρ:

ψ(x) = x − ρ xρ/ρ − 2π − ½ ln(1 − x−2)

Read it left to right. The first term, x, is the smooth guess. Then comes one term for every zero. The last two terms are tiny corrections. So everything interesting is in the terms for the zeros.

Write a zero as ρ = β + iγ. Then xρ = xβ · eiγ ln x, and the zero together with its complex conjugate β − iγ contributes a real oscillation:

  • its frequency is γ: the oscillation is cos(γ ln x), periodic on a ;
  • its amplitude is about 2xβ/γ, so it grows like xβ and higher zeros contribute less.

What a zero off the line would do

If every zero is on the , β = ½, then every wave has amplitude about √x, and the total gap is about √x: the bound from chapter 1. A single zero with β bigger than ½ would contribute a wave of size xβ, which eventually drowns out all the others. This is why the and the square-root bound on the gap are the same statement.

The mirror symmetry of chapter 2 matters here. A zero to the left of the line has a partner the same distance to the right, so any zero off the line produces a wave that grows faster than √x.
