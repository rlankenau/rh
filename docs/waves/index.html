Zeros as waves

The writes ψ(x) as x minus one oscillating term for each zero of ζ. A zero with β contributes a term of size about xβ, so the real parts of the zeros determine the size of ψ(x) − x.

Read it left to right. The first term, x, is the smooth guess. Then comes one term for every zero. The last two terms are tiny corrections. So everything interesting is in the terms for the zeros.

Write a zero as ρ = β + iγ. Then xρ = xβ · eiγ x, and the zero together with its complex conjugate β − iγ contributes a real oscillation:

  • its frequency is γ: the oscillation is cos(γ ln x), periodic on a ;
  • its amplitude is about 2xβ/γ, so it grows like xβ and higher zeros contribute less.

What a zero off the line would do

If every zero is on the , β = ½, then every wave has amplitude about √x, and the total gap is about √x: the bound from chapter 1. A single zero with β bigger than ½ would contribute a wave of size xβ, which eventually drowns out all the others. This is why the and the square-root bound on the gap are the same statement.

The mirror symmetry of chapter 5 matters here. A zero to the left of the line has a partner the same distance to the right, so any zero off the line produces a wave that grows faster than √x.

Exercises

  1. Show that xρ + xρ̄ = 2xβ cos(γ ln x) for ρ = β + iγ.
    Solution

    xρ = xβ eiγ ln x and xρ̄ = xβ e−iγ ln x. Their sum is xβ · 2 cos(γ ln x), because eiθ + e−iθ = 2 cos θ.

  2. By what factor must x grow for the wave of the first zero (γ ≈ 14.13) to complete one full oscillation?
    Solution

    cos(γ ln x) repeats when ln x increases by 2π/γ ≈ 0.445, that is, when x is multiplied by e0.445 ≈ 1.56.

  3. Two zeros have the same height, one with β = 0.5 and one with β = 0.6. What is the ratio of their amplitudes at x = 106?
    Solution

    x0.6/x0.5 = x0.1 = 100.6 ≈ 4. The ratio grows without bound as x does.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is "all zeros on the line σ = ½" the best possible case for the size of the error?

    • Because ½ is the smallest real part a zero can have
    • Because zeros come in mirror pairs β and 1 − β, so the largest real part is at least ½, with equality only if all are on the line
    • Because waves with β = ½ cancel each other

    The error is as large as its largest wave, xmax β. A zero with β < ½ forces a partner with 1 − β > ½, so nothing can beat √x.

  2. Each wave is cos(γ ln x). How do its wiggles look near x = 1,000,000 compared with near x = 1,000?

    • The same width
    • Narrower
    • A thousand times wider

    The wave is periodic in ln x: one cycle is a fixed ratio of x (1.56 for the first zero). So the width in x is proportional to x. This is why the labs use a logarithmic axis.

  3. What do the zeros at large height add to the reconstruction of the prime staircase?

    • Fine detail: the sharpness of each individual jump
    • The overall slope
    • Nothing; only the first few zeros matter

    A large γ is a high frequency. The first few zeros give the broad shape; resolving a single jump at a prime needs zeros with periods shorter than the gap between primes.
