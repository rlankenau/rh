Reading off the zeros

G(z) is the term from the real numbers. It is an explicit function with no poles in the half-plane Re z > 0.

Where the poles are

Consider the half-plane Re z > a, and ask where each term can have a pole.

  • Each fraction 1/(z − (ρ − ½)) has its pole at z = ρ − ½, whose is less than a. The paper shows that the whole sum is holomorphic for Re z > a.
  • 1/(z − ½) has a pole at z = ½, which corresponds to the pole of ζ at s = 1. The term 1/(z + ½) and G(z) have none there.
  • So ζ′(z + ½)/ζ(z + ½) has no pole with Re z > a other than z = ½.

But ζ′/ζ has a pole at of ζ. A zero ρ with Re ρ > ½ + a would give a pole at z = ρ − ½ with Re z > a. So there is no such zero. Since a > 0 was arbitrary, there is no zero with Re ρ > ½, and by the none with Re ρ < ½. That is the . The paper remarks that this argument does not use Weil's positivity criterion, the route that earlier -formula approaches aimed for.

What a pole looks like

Near a simple zero ρ, ζ′(s)/ζ(s) ≈ 1/(s − ρ). On a vertical line at distance d to the right of the zero, its peaks at about 1/d, at the height of the zero.

What would have to be checked

  • The divisor and its completion: that the completed has exactly the slots of the zeros inside the strip (chapters 12 and 13).
  • The computation of the in the completed spaces, without the classical (chapter 14).
  • The limit from allowed test functions to φz, uniformly enough in z to conclude that the limit is holomorphic.

By chapter 15 the trace formula with the restricted sum is equivalent to the hypothesis, so this last step is not where an error would hide: given the formula, the conclusion follows by the short argument above. The first two items carry the proof. Neither has been verified by anyone as of this writing, and this site has not checked them.

  2. Show that if f has a zero of N at ρ, then f′/f has a simple pole there with residue N.
    Solution

    Write f(s) = (s − ρ)N g(s) with g(ρ) ≠ 0. Then f′/f = N/(s − ρ) + g′/g, and g′/g is holomorphic near ρ. This is why the multiplicity N(ρ) appears as the coefficient in the formula.

  3. Suppose the formula were known only for one value, a = 0.2. What would it prove?
    Solution

    That ζ has no zero with Re ρ > 0.7, and by the functional equation none with Re ρ < 0.3. Any such zero-free region would already be a major result; the hypothesis itself needs every a > 0.

Check your understanding

  1. Why choose the test functions φz(t) = t−z?

    • They are the smoothest possible
    • They turn the prime sum into ζ′/ζ and each zero's term into a fraction 1/(z − (ρ − ½)), so the formula becomes a statement about poles
    • They vanish at the zeros

    With them, both sides are explicit functions of z, and one can compare where each side is allowed to be infinite.

  2. A zero on the critical line also gives a pole of ζ′/ζ. Why does it not contradict the conclusion?

    • Its pole is at Re z = 0, outside the half-plane Re z > a the argument is about
    • Zeros on the line give no poles
    • Its pole is cancelled by G(z)

    The argument says: no poles with Re z > a except z = ½. On-line zeros sit on the imaginary axis of the z-plane, to the left of that region, and they do appear in the sum on the right.

  3. Why must the formula hold for arbitrarily small a, not just for one value?

    • For a smaller a the formula is easier to prove
    • It need not
    • Each a only excludes zeros with Re ρ > ½ + a; zeros closer to the line are excluded only by letting a → 0

    One fixed a gives a zero-free region, not the hypothesis. The claim that the construction works for every a > 0 is essential.
