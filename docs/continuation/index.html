Analytic continuation

A formula can stop working at a point where the function it describes is perfectly well defined. Analytic continuation is the fact that an analytic function given on a small region extends to a larger one in at most one way. This chapter shows what that means for one example: the function 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) and its geometric series 1+x+x2+⋯1 + x + x^{2} + \cdots1+x+x2+⋯. Chapter 7 does it for ζ\zetaζ.

A function and a formula are different things

The geometric series 1+x+x2+x3+⋯1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + \cdots1+x+x2+x3+⋯ converges for −1<x<1-1 \lt x \lt 1−1<x<1, and there its sum is 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x). Outside that interval the series has no sum: at x=2x = 2x=2 its terms are 1, 2, 4, 8, …

The expression 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) has no such restriction. It is defined for every xxx except 1: at x=2x = 2x=2 it is −1, at x=−3x = -3x=−3 it is 14\tfrac1441​.

So there is one function, 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x), and the series is a way of computing it that works only on part of its domain. The series failing at x=2x = 2x=2 says something about the series, not about the function.

Why the extension is forced

Suppose all you knew was the geometric series, valid on (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1). Could some other function agree with it there and differ from 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) elsewhere?

Not an analytic one. By the rigidity of chapter 5, two analytic functions that agree on an interval agree everywhere that both are defined. So a function given on a small region has at most one analytic extension to a larger one.

That extension, when it exists, is the . It is not a convention or a choice of how to assign a value to a divergent sum. It is the same function, seen on more of its domain.

Extending step by step

The Taylor series of 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) about another center ccc is

11−x  =  ∑k≥0(x−c)k(1−c)k+1\frac{1}{1-x} \;=\; \sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{(x-c)^{k}}{(1-c)^{k+1}}1−x1​=k≥0∑​(1−c)k+1(x−c)k​ This is valid when ∣x−c∣<∣1−c∣|x - c| \lt |1 - c|∣x−c∣<∣1−c∣.

The series converges out to the distance from the center to the bad point x=1x = 1x=1, and no further. Starting from the original series (center 0, valid on (−1,1)(-1, 1)(−1,1)), compute the function and its derivatives at c=−0.9c = -0.9c=−0.9. The series about that center is valid on (−2.8,1)(-2.8, 1)(−2.8,1), which reaches points the original did not. Repeating this extends the function as far to the left as desired.

On the real line this process cannot get past x=1x = 1x=1. In the it can: a chain of overlapping discs can go around the bad point, and the function on the far side is still determined.

  3. The Taylor series of 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) about c=−3c = -3c=−3 converges on which interval? Does it give the value of the function at x=−6x = -6x=−6? At x=2x = 2x=2?
    Solution

  1. The geometric series diverges at x=2x = 2x=2, and 1/(1−x)1/(1-x)1/(1−x) equals −1 there. Which statement is right?

    • The function has the value −1; the series is a way of computing the function that works only for ∣x∣<1|x| \lt 1∣x∣<1
    • The function is undefined at 2

    The sum 1+2+4+⋯1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots1+2+4+⋯ has no value. The function it came from does, and analytic continuation is about the function.

  2. Why is there only one analytic continuation?

    • Two analytic functions that agree on any interval agree wherever both are defined
    • Mathematicians agreed on a convention
    • Because the series converges everywhere

    Rigidity: the values near one point fix every Taylor coefficient, and so the whole function. This is what makes it legitimate to speak of ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) where the sum diverges.

    The radius is the distance from the center to the bad point. Moving the center left makes the radius larger, so the left end moves, but the right end is always the bad point itself.
