Part I · Chapter 1

Counting primes

The is equivalent to a bound on the error in the standard estimate for the number of up to xxx. This chapter defines the count, the estimate, and the error.

The staircase and its smooth guess

Write π(x)\pi(x)π(x) for the number of primes up to xxx. It is a staircase: flat, then up by one at each prime. Primes thin out as numbers get bigger, and noticed the rule: near a large number xxx, about one number in every ln⁡x\ln xlnx is prime. Around a thousand that is one in 7; around a million, one in 14.

If the chance of being prime near ttt is 1/ln⁡t1/\ln t1/lnt, then adding up those chances for every ttt from 2 to xxx should estimate how many primes there are up to xxx. That running total is called the , written li⁡(x)=∫2xdtln⁡t\operatorname{li}(x) = \int_{2}^{x} \frac{dt}{\ln t}li(x)=∫2x​lntdt​. A cruder estimate, x/ln⁡xx/\ln xx/lnx, has the right shape but runs low.

Weighing primes instead of counting them

Counting each prime as 1 turns out to give awkward formulas. Number theorists get cleaner ones from a . Give every pkp^{k}pk the weight ln⁡p\ln plnp, and every other number weight zero. This weight is called Λ(n)\Lambda(n)Λ(n), the , and its running total is 's function, written ψ(x)=∑n≤xΛ(n)\psi(x) = \sum_{n \le x} \Lambda(n)ψ(x)=∑n≤x​Λ(n): add up the weights of all whole numbers up to xxx.

The weights exactly cancel the thinning-out. Primes near xxx are rarer by a factor of ln⁡x\ln xlnx, but each one counts for ln⁡x\ln xlnx, so the weighted total should grow like xxx itself. The says that it does: ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) divided by xxx gets closer and closer to 1.

The error is the whole question

How far does the staircase stray from the line? If primes were placed by fair coin flips with the right density, the error up to xxx would be about x\sqrt{x}x​ in size. The Hypothesis is equivalent to saying the primes do essentially that well:

The Riemann Hypothesis is true exactly when the gap between ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) and xxx is never more than about x\sqrt{x}x​. Precisely: the gap is at most C⋅x⋅(ln⁡x)2C \cdot \sqrt{x} \cdot (\ln x)^{2}C⋅x​⋅(lnx)2 for some fixed number CCC. What has actually been proved so far is much weaker: the gap is known to be smaller than xxx, but not smaller than any fixed root of xxx.

  1. Compute ψ(10)\psi(10)ψ(10) by hand.
    Solution

    The prime powers up to 10 are 2, 3, 4, 5, 7, 8, 9. The weights are ln⁡2\ln 2ln2 three times (for 2, 4, 8), ln⁡3\ln 3ln3 twice (for 3, 9), ln⁡5\ln 5ln5 and ln⁡7\ln 7ln7. Check your understanding

  1. Why does ψ\psiψ give a prime ppp the weight ln⁡p\ln plnp instead of counting it as 1?

    • So that the sum is always a whole number
    • Near xxx about one number in ln⁡x\ln xlnx is prime, so weights of ln⁡p\ln plnp make the total grow like xxx itself
    • Because large primes matter less than small ones
    • So that the sum converges

    The weight cancels the thinning-out of the primes. The comparison function for ψ\psiψ is then simply xxx, where for π\piπ it is the integral li⁡(x)\operatorname{li}(x)li(x). Note the weight makes large primes count more.

  2. The prime number theorem says ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x) is "close to" xxx. Which reading is correct?

    • ψ(x)−x\psi(x) - xψ(x)−x stays below some fixed number
    • ψ(x)−x\psi(x) - xψ(x)−x eventually becomes 0

    The theorem controls the error only relative to xxx. The difference ψ(x)−x\psi(x) - xψ(x)−x is unbounded; how fast it can grow is exactly what the Riemann Hypothesis is about.

    • More, because the numbers are larger
