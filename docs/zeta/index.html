The zeta function

One function packages every at once. The inputs where it equals zero control how far the prime staircase strays from its smooth guess, and the says where those inputs are.

A sum that is also a product

Pick a number s bigger than 1. The is the endless sum ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + …, one term for every whole number. For example ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + … ≈ 1.645. found that the same number is a :

ζ(s) = p prime 1 / (1 − p−s). Multiply the factors out and every whole number appears exactly once, because every whole number factors into primes in exactly one way.

This identity is the bridge between two worlds. The sum knows only about whole numbers; the product knows only about primes. Because they are equal, anything learned about the smooth function ζ is a fact about primes. In particular, some algebra turns the product into a formula for the weighted prime count of chapter 1, and in that formula the inputs where ζ equals zero play the leading role.

Complex inputs and the critical strip

's step was to let the input s be a : a point on a flat map instead of a point on a line. It is written s = σ + it, where σ says how far right the point is and t says how far up (its "height"). The output ζ(s) is then also a point on a map.

The Riemann Hypothesis: every zero in the critical strip is on the critical line. Because zeros come in mirror pairs, this is the same as saying there are none to the right of the line, with σ > ½.

Watching a zero happen

The output of ζ is a point on a map, and it is zero only when that point sits exactly at the centre of the map (the origin). Follow the output ζ(σ + it) as the height t climbs: it loops around, and a zero is a loop that passes exactly through the origin.

How the lab computes ζ

The defining sum does not settle on a value inside the critical strip, so the lab uses a method due to that does, with 70 terms. Checked against published values it is accurate to about six decimal places for heights up to 60, which is why the labs stop there.
