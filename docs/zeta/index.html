The zeta function

The is the sum ζ(s)=1+1/2s+1/3s+1/4s+⋯\zeta(s) = 1 + 1/2^{s} + 1/3^{s} + 1/4^{s} + \cdotsζ(s)=1+1/2s+1/3s+1/4s+⋯ This chapter treats it for real numbers sss only: when the sum has a value, and why it equals a product over the .

The sum

Fix a real number sss and add up 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns over the whole numbers n=1,2,3,…n = 1, 2, 3, \dotsn=1,2,3,… For s=2s = 2s=2 the terms are 1,1/4,1/9,1/16,…1, 1/4, 1/9, 1/16, \dots1,1/4,1/9,1/16,…, and the sum is 1.6449… ( showed it is exactly π2/6\pi^{2}/6π2/6). The larger sss is, the faster the terms shrink.

For s=1s = 1s=1 the terms are 1,1/2,1/3,1/4,…1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, \dots1,1/2,1/3,1/4,… They shrink, but too slowly: the sum of the first NNN terms is about ln⁡N+0.577\ln N + 0.577lnN+0.577, which grows without bound. This is the harmonic series, and it has no finite sum.

The dividing line is s=1s = 1s=1. To see why, compare each term with an area. The term 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns is the area of a rectangle of width 1 and height 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns. Place that rectangle on the interval from n−1n - 1n−1 to nnn. The curve y=1/xsy = 1/x^{s}y=1/xs is decreasing, so on that interval it lies above the rectangle, and the area under the curve is larger:

1ns  ≤  ∫n−1ndxxs\frac{1}{n^{s}} \;\le\; \int_{n-1}^{n} \frac{dx}{x^{s}}ns1​≤∫n−1n​xsdx​

Add this up over n=N+1,N+2,…n = N + 1, N + 2, \dotsn=N+1,N+2,… The intervals join up into everything to the right of NNN:

∑n>N1ns  ≤  ∫N∞dxxs  =  N1−ss−1when s>1\sum_{n>N} \frac{1}{n^{s}} \;\le\; \int_{N}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^{s}} \;=\; \frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1} \qquad \text{when } s>1n>N∑​ns1​≤∫N∞​xsdx​=s−1N1−s​when s>1

So for s>1s \gt 1s>1 the terms after the NNN-th add up to at most N1−s/(s−1)N^{1 - s}/(s - 1)N1−s/(s−1), which shrinks to 0 as NNN grows. The sum converges.

Placing each rectangle one step to the right, on the interval from nnn to n+1n + 1n+1, puts it above the curve and gives the opposite inequality: the terms from the NNN-th on add up to at least the same . For s≤1s \le 1s≤1 that integral is infinite, so the sum is too. The two bounds differ by less than a single term, so the integral is also a good estimate of what the remaining terms add up to.

So ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is defined, for now, only for s>1s \gt 1s>1.

Near s=1s = 1s=1

As sss decreases toward 1 the sum grows without bound. The integral above with N=1N = 1N=1 says how: ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is close to 1/(s−1)1/(s - 1)1/(s−1). More precisely, ζ(s)−1/(s−1)\zeta(s) - 1/(s - 1)ζ(s)−1/(s−1) tends to 0.5772…, a number called .

The product over primes

Recall the geometric series: for ∣x∣<1|x| \lt 1∣x∣<1, 1+x+x2+x3+⋯=1/(1−x)1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + \cdots = 1/(1 - x)1+x+x2+x3+⋯=1/(1−x). Put x=1/2sx = 1/2^{s}x=1/2s:

11−2−s  =  1+12s+14s+18s+⋯\frac{1}{1-2^{-s}} \;=\; 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\frac{1}{8^{s}}+\cdots1−2−s1​=1+2s1​+4s1​+8s1​+⋯ This is the part of the zeta sum that runs over the powers of 2.

Do the same for 3 and multiply the two series together:

11−2−s⋅11−3−s  =  (1+12s+14s+⋯ )(1+13s+19s+⋯ )\frac{1}{1-2^{-s}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-3^{-s}} \;=\; \left(1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\cdots\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{3^{s}}+\frac{1}{9^{s}}+\cdots\right)1−2−s1​⋅1−3−s1​=(1+2s1​+4s1​+⋯)(1+3s1​+9s1​+⋯) =  1+12s+13s+14s+16s+18s+19s+112s+⋯=\; 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{3^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\frac{1}{6^{s}}+\frac{1}{8^{s}}+\frac{1}{9^{s}}+\frac{1}{12^{s}}+\cdots=1+2s1​+3s1​+4s1​+6s1​+8s1​+9s1​+12s1​+⋯

Each product of a term from the first bracket and a term from the second is 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns for n=2a3bn = 2^{a}3^{b}n=2a3b, and each such nnn occurs once. The numbers 5, 7, 10, 11, … are missing because they have a prime factor other than 2 and 3. Include a factor for every prime, and every whole number appears exactly once, because every whole number is a product of primes in exactly one way. This is Euler's product formula:

ζ(s)  =  ∏p prime11−p−sfor s>1\zeta(s) \;=\; \prod_{p\ \text{prime}} \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}} \qquad \text{for } s>1ζ(s)=p prime∏​1−p−s1​for s>1

Three consequences

  • ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is never 0 for s>1s \gt 1s>1. Every factor of the product is greater than 1.
  • There are infinitely many primes. If there were finitely many, the product would have a finite value at s=1s = 1s=1. But as sss decreases to 1 the sum grows without bound.
  • The weights of chapter 1 appear. Take the logarithm of the product, ln⁡ζ(s)=−∑pln⁡ ⁣(1−p−s)\displaystyle \ln\zeta(s) = -\sum_{p}\ln\!\left(1-p^{-s}\right)lnζ(s)=−p∑​ln(1−p−s), and differentiate:

−ζ′(s)ζ(s)  =  ∑nΛ(n)ns-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} \;=\; \sum_{n} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}−ζ(s)ζ′(s)​=n∑​nsΛ(n)​ Here Λ(n)=ln⁡p\Lambda(n) = \ln pΛ(n)=lnp if nnn is a power of the prime ppp, and 0 otherwise.

The left side is built from ζ\zetaζ alone. The right side is a sum over with the same weights that define ψ(x)\psi(x)ψ(x). Exercise 2 derives it.

Check your understanding

  1. The terms 1/n1/n1/n shrink to 0, yet the harmonic series has no finite sum. What decides whether ∑1/ns\sum 1/n^{s}∑1/ns converges?

    • Whether the terms tend to 0
    • How fast they shrink: the tail behaves like ∫dx/xs\int dx/x^{s}∫dx/xs, which is finite only for s>1s \gt 1s>1
    • Whether sss is a whole number

    Terms tending to 0 is necessary but not enough. At s=1s = 1s=1 the first NNN terms add up to about ln⁡N\ln NlnN, which is unbounded.

  2. The product using only the primes 2 and 3 is, multiplied out, a sum of 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns over which nnn?

    • All nnn up to 3
    • The multiples of 2 or 3
    • The nnn with no prime factor other than 2 and 3: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, …

    Each term of the expanded product is 1/(2a3b)s1/(2^{a}3^{b})^{s}1/(2a3b)s. 10 is a multiple of 2 but is missing, because it also needs the prime 5.

  3. How does the product formula show there are infinitely many primes?

    • With finitely many primes the product would stay finite as s→1s \to 1s→1, but the sum grows without bound there
    • The product has infinitely many factors by definition
    • It does not; that needs a separate argument

    Sum and product are equal for every s>1s \gt 1s>1. A finite product of the factors 1/(1−p−s)1/(1 - p^{-s})1/(1−p−s) tends to a finite number as s→1s \to 1s→1, while the sum behaves like 1/(s−1)1/(s - 1)1/(s−1). This was Euler's proof.
