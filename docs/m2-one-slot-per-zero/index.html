One slot per zero

Take the smooth functions and regard two as equal when they differ by a . The paper's first main claim is that an element of the resulting space is determined by one number for each zero of the . The acts on this space, and its are the values of M(φ)M(\varphi)M(φ) at the zeros.

Based on §1.2.1 of the paper (pages 13–14), read in full. The identification of the quotient with the zeros rests on the paper's "Structure Theorems", which I have not read.

The quotient HHH

The paper's first structural claim combines chapters 10 and 11. Let SSS be the space of smooth, rapidly decaying functions on the group where Θ\ThetaΘ is defined. The θ-functions form a subspace of SSS. Form the quotient space HHH: elements of SSS, with two regarded as equal when they differ by a θ-function.

  • The transforms of elements of SSS are of sss.
  • The transforms of θ-functions are the multiples of the L-function, shifted by ½. In the zeta case there is an extra factor (s+12)(s−12)(s + \tfrac12)(s - \tfrac12)(s+21​)(s−21​) that cancels .
  • So an element of HHH is determined by the values of its transform at the zeros, together with derivatives at of higher : one slot per zero, counted with multiplicity.

Masullo calls 0→θ-functions→S→H→00 \to \theta\text{-functions} \to S \to H \to 00→θ-functions→S→H→0 the fundamental divisor . It has the same form as a standard fact in geometry: functions modulo those that vanish on a set of points correspond to functions on that set of points. He credits the idea to . What he claims as new is keeping SSS and HHH as of smooth functions, where earlier work passed immediately to square-integrable functions.

Lab 11.1 shows one direction: the transform of every θ-function is a multiple of ζ\zetaζ. The claim also needs the converse, that every suitable multiple of the L-function is the transform of a θ-function, and a statement about the topology of HHH. The paper says this "requires all the Structure Theorems" plus known facts about L-functions (the , and control of 1/L1/L1/L on a sequence of horizontal lines). Those theorems take up a substantial part of the 940 pages. I have not checked them.

The difficulty

The proof uses test functions φ\varphiφ that are zero for t≤1t \le 1t≤1. By property 2 of chapter 9, for a zero ρ=β+iγ\rho = \beta + i\gammaρ=β+iγ the eigenvalue M(φ)(ρ−12)M(\varphi)(\rho - \tfrac12)M(φ)(ρ−21​) involves the factor ∣tρ−1/2∣=tβ−1/2|t^{\rho - 1/2}| = t^{\beta - 1/2}∣tρ−1/2∣=tβ−1/2, which for t>1t \gt 1t>1 grows with β\betaβ. So the eigenvalue is larger for a zero to the right of the critical line than for its mirror image to the left. Nothing so far shows that the sum over all zeros converges, or that it is the trace of an operator on a .

Things to try

  • Narrow φ\varphiφ. Its transform decays more slowly, so more zeros have eigenvalues of noticeable size.
  • Move the center of φ\varphiφ. The sizes of the eigenvalues barely change, but the trace does: it depends on their phases.

Check your understanding

  1. The lab of the previous chapter shows that the transform of a θ-function is a multiple of ζ\zetaζ. Why is the converse also needed for "one slot per zero"?

    • It is not needed
    • If some multiples of ζ\zetaζ were not transforms of θ-functions, the quotient HHH would be larger than one slot per zero
    • Because otherwise HHH would be empty

    HHH is SSS modulo θ-functions. Dividing by a smaller subspace leaves a bigger quotient, with extra elements unrelated to the zeros. The paper's Structure Theorems are what rule that out.

  2. For a test function supported at t>1t \gt 1t>1, compare the eigenvalue of a hypothetical zero with β=0.8\beta = 0.8β=0.8 with that of its mirror image at β=0.2\beta = 0.2β=0.2.

    • Equal in size
    • The one for 0.2 is larger
    • The one for 0.8 is larger, because tβ−1/2t^{\beta - 1/2}tβ−1/2 is larger for t>1t \gt 1t>1

    The eigenvalue is M(φ)(ρ−12)M(\varphi)(\rho - \tfrac12)M(φ)(ρ−21​), an integral against tρ−1/2t^{\rho - 1/2}tρ−1/2. Zeros to the right of the line are amplified, which is why the full sum over zeros is not obviously the trace of a well-behaved operator.

  3. Why is A(φ)A(\varphi)A(φ) allowed to act on the quotient HHH at all?

    • Because it sends θ-functions to θ-functions, so "equal up to a θ-function" is preserved
    • Because it sends every function to a θ-function
    • Because HHH is finite-dimensional

    An operator passes to a quotient exactly when it maps the subspace being divided out into itself. On transforms: multiplying a multiple of ζ\zetaζ by M(φ)M(\varphi)M(φ) gives a multiple of ζ\zetaζ.
