Complex inputs

Why Riemann wanted complex s

In 1859 Riemann wrote one short paper on number theory, "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude". had an approximation for the number of up to x (chapter 1). Riemann wanted an exact formula.

The link between ζ and the primes at the end of chapter 2 can be written as an :

−ζ′(s)ζ(s)  =  s∫1∞ψ(x) x−s−1 dx-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} \;=\; s\int_{1}^{\infty} \psi(x)\, x^{-s-1}\, dx−ζ(s)ζ′(s)​=s∫1∞​ψ(x)x−s−1dx

This gives a function of s in terms of the prime-counting function ψ. Riemann wanted the reverse: ψ in terms of ζ. The integral is a transform of the same kind as the and Laplace transforms, and the formula that inverts such a transform is an integral over complex values of s. So to solve for the primes, ζ has to be understood as a function of a complex variable. Riemann was the leading expert of his time on such functions, and he carried the inversion through.

Complex numbers in three facts

  • A complex number z = a + bi is a point (a, b) , or the arrow from the origin to that point. a is its real part and b its imaginary part.
  • Its |z| = √(a² + b²) is the length of the arrow. z = 0 only when both parts are 0.
  • 's formula: eiθ = cos θ + i sin θ. This is the point on the unit circle at angle θ. Multiplying a number by eiθ rotates its arrow by the angle θ without changing its length.

What 1/ns means

Write s = σ + it, with σ the real part and t the imaginary part. Since n = e n,

n−s  =  e−sln⁡n  =  n−σ⋅e−itln⁡nn^{-s} \;=\; e^{-s\ln n} \;=\; n^{-\sigma}\cdot e^{-it\ln n}n−s=e−slnn=n−σ⋅e−itlnn This is an arrow of length n−σ, at the angle −t ln n.

The real part σ sets the length, exactly as for real s. The imaginary part t does not change the length at all. It only turns the arrow, and it turns the arrow for a large n further than the arrow for a small n.

Adding the arrows

For complex s the definition is the same sum, ζ(s) = n−s. Complex numbers are added by placing the arrows head to tail. The sum of the first N terms is where the chain of N arrows ends.

The total length of the chain is Σ n−σ, which is the real sum of chapter 2 and is finite when σ > 1. A chain of finite total length must close in on a single point. So:

For every complex s with real part σ > 1, the sum converges, and its value ζ(s) is a complex number. Euler's product also holds there, so ζ(s) ≠ 0 for σ > 1.

Exercises

  1. Compute 2−s for s = 1 + iπ/ln 2.
    Solution

    The length is 2−1 = ½ and the angle is −t ln 2 = −π, a half turn. So 2−s = ½ · e−iπ = −½.

  2. Show that |n−s| = n−σ, whatever t is.
    Solution

    n−s = n−σ · e−it ln n, and |eiθ| = √(cos² θ + sin² θ) = 1 for every real θ.

  3. The chain for s = 2 + 5i has what total length?
    Solution

    The lengths are n−2, so the total is ζ(2) = π²/6 ≈ 1.645. The chain ends at ζ(2 + 5i), which is at most that far from the origin, since a chain cannot end further away than its own length.

Check your understanding

  1. In s = σ + it, what does changing t do to the term n−s?

    • It changes the length of the arrow
    • Nothing
    • It rotates the arrow without changing its length, and rotates arrows with larger n faster

    The angle is −t ln n and the length is n−σ. All the difference between real and complex s is in how the arrows are turned relative to each other.

  2. Why does the sum converge for every t once σ > 1?

    • The chain of arrows has finite total length Σ n−σ, and turning the arrows cannot make it longer
    • Because the arrows cancel each other
    • Because t is small

    Convergence here does not depend on cancellation. When σ ≤ 1 the total length is infinite, and whether the chain settles then depends entirely on the turning.

  3. Why did Riemann need complex s at all, when the primes and the sum are real?

    • Because primes are complex numbers
    • −ζ′/ζ is an integral transform of the prime-counting function, and the formula that inverts such a transform integrates over complex s
    • To make the sum converge faster

    The same happens with the Fourier transform: a real signal is recovered from its transform by integrating over frequencies with complex exponentials.
