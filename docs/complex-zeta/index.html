Complex inputs

Why Riemann wanted complex sss

In 1859 Riemann wrote one short paper on number theory, "On the Number of Primes Less Than a Given Magnitude". had an approximation for the number of up to xxx (chapter 1). Riemann wanted an exact formula.

The link between ζ\zetaζ and the primes at the end of chapter 2 can be written as an :

−ζ′(s)ζ(s)  =  s∫1∞ψ(x) x−s−1 dx-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} \;=\; s\int_{1}^{\infty} \psi(x)\, x^{-s-1}\, dx−ζ(s)ζ′(s)​=s∫1∞​ψ(x)x−s−1dx

This gives a function of sss in terms of the prime-counting function ψ\psiψ. Riemann wanted the reverse: ψ\psiψ in terms of ζ\zetaζ. The integral is a transform of the same kind as the and Laplace transforms, and the formula that inverts such a transform is an integral over complex values of sss. So to solve for the primes, ζ\zetaζ has to be understood as a function of a complex variable. Riemann was the leading expert of his time on such functions, and he carried the inversion through.

What 1/ns1/n^{s}1/ns means

Write s=σ+its = \sigma + its=σ+it, with σ\sigmaσ the real part and ttt the imaginary part. Since n=eln⁡nn = e^{\ln n}n=elnn,

n−s  =  e−sln⁡n  =  n−σ⋅e−itln⁡nn^{-s} \;=\; e^{-s\ln n} \;=\; n^{-\sigma}\cdot e^{-it\ln n}n−s=e−slnn=n−σ⋅e−itlnn This is a vector of length n−σn^{-\sigma}n−σ, at the angle −tln⁡n-t \ln n−tlnn.

The real part σ\sigmaσ sets the length, exactly as for real sss. The imaginary part ttt does not change the length at all. It only turns the vector, and it turns the vector for a large nnn further than the vector for a small nnn.

Adding the vectors

For complex sss the definition is the same sum, ζ(s)=∑n−s\zeta(s) = \sum n^{-s}ζ(s)=∑n−s. Complex numbers are added by placing the vectors head to tail. The sum of the first NNN terms is where the chain of NNN vectors ends.

The total length of the chain is ∑n−σ\sum n^{-\sigma}∑n−σ, which is the real sum of chapter 2 and is finite when σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1. A chain of finite total length must close in on a single point. So:

For every complex sss with real part σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1, the sum converges, and its value ζ(s)\zeta(s)ζ(s) is a complex number. Euler's product also holds there, so ζ(s)≠0\zeta(s) \ne 0ζ(s)=0 for σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1.

Check your understanding

  1. In s=σ+its = \sigma + its=σ+it, what does changing ttt do to the term n−sn^{-s}n−s?

    • It changes the length of the vector
    • Nothing
    • It rotates the vector without changing its length, and rotates vectors with larger nnn faster

    The angle is −tln⁡n-t \ln n−tlnn and the length is n−σn^{-\sigma}n−σ. All the difference between real and complex sss is in how the vectors are turned relative to each other.

  2. Why does the sum converge for every ttt once σ>1\sigma \gt 1σ>1?

    • The chain of vectors has finite total length ∑n−σ\sum n^{-\sigma}∑n−σ, and turning the vectors cannot make it longer
    • Because the vectors cancel each other
    • Because ttt is small

    Convergence here does not depend on cancellation. When σ≤1\sigma \le 1σ≤1 the total length is infinite, and whether the chain settles then depends entirely on the turning.

  3. Why did Riemann need complex sss at all, when the primes and the sum are real?

    • Because primes are complex numbers
    • −ζ′/ζ-\zeta'/\zeta−ζ′/ζ is an integral transform of the prime-counting function, and the formula that inverts such a transform integrates over complex sss
    • To make the sum converge faster

    The same happens with the Fourier transform: a real signal is recovered from its transform by integrating over frequencies with complex exponentials.
