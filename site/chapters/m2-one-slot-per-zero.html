One slot per zero

In chapter M1 the trace was a sum over the Fourier modes of a circle. This chapter constructs a quotient space of functions whose coordinates are indexed by the zeros of ζ, so that the trace of the same convolution operator on it is a sum over zeros.

Based on §1.1.4, §1.2.1 and §1.2.8 of the paper (pages 12–17), read in full. The identification of the quotient space with the zeros rests on the paper's "Structure Theorems", which I have not read. The labs work over the ordinary integers with the trivial character, which is the Riemann zeta case; the paper works over any number field. The Lean file RH/Slots.lean proves the toy fact in the first lab.

The Mellin transform

The Mellin transform of a function f on (0, ∞) is M(f)(s) = ∫0∞ f(t) ts dt/t, a function of a complex number s. The substitution t = eu turns it into ∫ f(eu) esu du, which for s = iy is the Fourier transform in the variable u = ln t. The property used below is that rescaling becomes multiplication: the transform of f(γt) is γ−s · M(f)(s).

On the circle

On the circle of chapter M1, t and γt are the same point. A function ϕ on (0, ∞) gives a function on the circle by summing over all the points that are identified: Φγ(ϕ)(t) = Σk ϕ(γkt), over all integers k.

Φγ sends some nonzero functions to 0. The simplest is a difference ϕ(t) − ϕ(γt): the sum over k telescopes. By the rescaling property its Mellin transform is (1 − γ−s) · M(ϕ)(s), and the factor 1 − γ−s is zero exactly at s = 2πim/ln γ, the Fourier modes of the circle. The paper states that every function sent to 0 is of this kind:

Φγ(ϕ) = 0 exactly when M(ϕ)(s) is 1 − γ−s times an entire function. So a function on the circle corresponds to a transform modulo multiples of 1 − γ−s. Two transforms agree modulo those multiples exactly when they have the same values at the zeros of 1 − γ−s. A function on the circle is therefore determined by one number for each mode m: one slot per zero of 1 − γ−s.

Summing over whole numbers

The paper uses the same construction with a different sum, the one at the centre of Tate's thesis. Take a function f on (0, ∞) and sum its values at all whole-number multiples of x:

Θf(x) = √x · Σn ≥ 1 f(nx). The paper calls functions of this form θ-functions. The Mellin transform is M(Θf)(s) = ζ(s + ½) · M(f)(s + ½).

Proof: by the rescaling property the transform of f(nx) is n−s · M(f)(s), and Σ n−s = ζ(s). Multiplying by √x replaces s by s + ½. (The sum converges for Re s > ½; the identity extends to all s by analytic continuation when Θf decays at both ends.) The factor ζ here plays the role that 1 − γ−s played on the circle.

So the transform of every θ-function is zero at the zeros of ζ, whatever f is. Because of the shift by ½, a zero ρ of ζ appears at s = ρ − ½. If the Riemann Hypothesis holds these points are all on the imaginary axis, at s = ±14.13i, ±21.02i, ±25.01i, …

The divisor exact sequence

The paper's first structural claim combines the two sections above. Let S be the space of smooth, rapidly decaying functions on the group where Θ is defined. The θ-functions form a subspace of S. Form the quotient space H: elements of S, with two regarded as equal when they differ by a θ-function.

  • The Mellin transforms of elements of S are entire functions of s.
  • The transforms of θ-functions are the multiples of the L-function, shifted by ½. In the zeta case there is an extra factor (s + ½)(s − ½) that cancels its pole.
  • So an element of H is determined by the values of its transform at the zeros, together with derivatives at a zero of higher multiplicity: one slot per zero, counted with multiplicity.

Masullo calls 0 → θ-functions → S → H → 0 the fundamental divisor exact sequence. It has the same form as a standard fact in geometry: functions modulo those that vanish on a set of points correspond to functions on that set of points. He credits the idea to Connes. What he claims as new is keeping S and H as topological spaces of smooth functions, where earlier work passed immediately to square-integrable functions.

The lab shows one direction: the transform of every θ-function is a multiple of ζ. The claim also needs the converse, that every suitable multiple of the L-function is the transform of a θ-function, and a statement about the topology of H. The paper says this "requires all the Structure Theorems" plus known facts about L-functions (the convexity bound, and control of 1/L on a sequence of horizontal lines). Those theorems take up a substantial part of the 940 pages. I have not checked them.

The convolution operator on H

The convolution operator A(φ) of chapter M1 sends θ-functions to θ-functions, so it acts on the quotient H. On transforms it is multiplication by M(φ)(s), so on the slot belonging to a zero ρ it multiplies by the number M(φ)(ρ − ½). Its trace is the sum of those numbers:

trace = Σρ N(ρ) · M(φ)(ρ − ½), where N(ρ) is the multiplicity of the zero. This is the spectral side of the trace formula: the sum over modes in chapter M1, with zeros of ζ in place of zeros of 1 − γ−s.

There is a difficulty. The proof uses test functions φ that are zero for t ≤ 1. For a zero ρ = β + iγ, |tρ − ½| = tβ − ½, which for t > 1 grows with β. So M(φ)(ρ − ½) is larger for a zero to the right of the critical line than for its mirror image to the left, and nothing so far shows that the sum over all zeros is the trace of an operator on a Hilbert space. Masullo's answer is to measure functions with a new norm, under which only the slots near the critical line remain.

In Lean

The statement of Lab M2.1 is proved in full (no sorry) against Mathlib:

theorem mellin_sub_scaled_eq_zero {γ : ℝ} (hγ : 1 < γ) {φ : ℝ → ℂ} (m : ℤ)
    (hφ : MellinConvergent φ (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ)) :
    mellin (fun t => φ t - φ (γ * t)) (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ) = 0

The matching statement for Lab M2.2, that the transform of Σ f(nx) is ζ times the transform of f, is not yet formalized here; Mathlib has the Mellin transform and riemannZeta but not this identity in this form.
