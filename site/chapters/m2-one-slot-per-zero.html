One slot per zero

In chapter M1 the trace was a sum over the frequencies of a circle. In the real proof the frequencies are the zeros of an L-function. This chapter is about how zeros get to play that role: by building a space of functions in which each zero is one coordinate.

Based on §1.1.4, §1.2.1 and §1.2.8 of the paper (pages 12–17), read in full. The identification of the quotient space with the zeros rests on the paper's "Structure Theorems", which I have not read. The labs work over the ordinary integers with the trivial character, which is the Riemann zeta case; the paper works over any number field. The Lean file RH/Slots.lean proves the toy fact in the first lab.

The Mellin transform

The tool throughout is the Mellin transform, M(f)(s) = ∫0∞ f(t) ts dt/t. It is the Fourier transform read on a logarithmic ruler: it takes a function on the positive reals and returns a function of a complex number s, and it turns scaling into multiplication. Stretching f by a factor γ multiplies its transform by γ−s.

The toy version

Go back to the circle of chapter M1, where t and γt are the same point. A function on the positive reals becomes a function on the circle by adding up its values at all the points that were glued together: Φγ(ϕ)(t) = Σk ϕ(γkt).

Some functions wrap up to nothing. The simplest is a difference ϕ(t) − ϕ(γt): its wrapped sum telescopes to zero. On the Mellin side that difference is (1 − γ−s) · M(ϕ)(s), and the factor 1 − γ−s is zero exactly at the circle's frequencies s = 2πim/ln γ. The paper states that this is the whole story:

A function wraps to zero on the circle exactly when its Mellin transform is divisible by 1 − γ−s. So a function on the circle is the same thing as a transform modulo multiples of 1 − γ−s, and what survives of a transform modulo those multiples is its list of values at the zeros of 1 − γ−s. One slot per frequency.

The real version: summing over whole numbers

Now replace "wrap around a circle" with the operation at the heart of Tate's thesis. Take a function f on the real line and add up its values along every multiple of x:

Θf(x) = √x · Σn ≥ 1 f(nx). The paper calls functions of this kind θ-functions. Its transform is M(Θf)(s) = ζ(s + ½) · M(f)(s + ½).

The reason is one line: the term f(nx) is f stretched by n, so its transform is n−s times that of f, and Σ n−s is zeta. The sum over whole numbers has put ζ in front of the transform, in the same way that the difference put 1 − γ−s in front in the toy.

The consequence is the point of the chapter. Every θ-function, whatever f it came from, has a transform that vanishes at the zeros of zeta. The factor √x shifts everything by ½, so a zero ρ of zeta shows up at s = ρ − ½. On RH those points all lie on the imaginary axis, at heights 14.13, 21.02, 25.01…

The divisor exact sequence

The paper's first structural claim puts the two halves together. Start with the space of all rapidly decaying smooth functions on the group where Θ lives. Inside it sits the subspace of θ-functions. Take the quotient: functions, with any two declared equal when they differ by a θ-function.

  • On the Mellin side the big space is a space of entire functions of s.
  • The θ-functions are the multiples of the L-function (shifted by ½, and with an extra factor (s + ½)(s − ½) in the zeta case to cancel its pole).
  • So the quotient remembers of each transform only its values at the zeros, plus derivatives at a zero of higher multiplicity: one slot per zero, counted with multiplicity.

Masullo calls this the fundamental divisor exact sequence, because it has the same shape as a standard fact in geometry: functions, modulo functions that vanish on a set of points, are the same as functions on that set of points. He credits the picture to Connes and says the new part is taking the function spaces seriously as topological spaces rather than passing at once to square-integrable functions.

The lab shows one direction: every θ-function's transform is a multiple of zeta. The claim needs the converse too, that every suitable multiple of the L-function arises this way, and that the quotient has the right topology. The paper says this "requires all the Structure Theorems" plus known facts about L-functions (the convexity bound, and control of 1/L on a sequence of horizontal lines). That is a substantial part of the 940 pages and I have not checked it.

What the smearing operator does to a slot

Take the smearing operator A(φ) from chapter M1: average a function against a test bump φ. It sends θ-functions to θ-functions, so it acts on the quotient, slot by slot. On the slot belonging to a zero ρ it multiplies by the single number M(φ)(ρ − ½). Its trace is the sum of those numbers:

trace = Σρ N(ρ) · M(φ)(ρ − ½), where N(ρ) is the multiplicity of the zero. This is the spectral side of the trace formula, and it is exactly the toy's sum over frequencies with zeros in place of frequencies.

This is where a real difficulty appears. The proof uses bumps φ that live entirely on t > 1. For such a bump, M(φ)(ρ − ½) is large when ρ is to the right of the critical line and small when it is to the left. A sum over all zeros treats a possible off-line zero and its mirror image very differently, and there is no reason yet for the trace to exist as the trace of an operator on a Hilbert space. Masullo's answer is to measure the size of functions in a new way, a new norm, under which only the slots near the critical line survive.

In Lean

The toy fact of Lab M2.1 is proved in full (no sorry) against Mathlib:

theorem mellin_sub_scaled_eq_zero {γ : ℝ} (hγ : 1 < γ) {φ : ℝ → ℂ} (m : ℤ)
    (hφ : MellinConvergent φ (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ)) :
    mellin (fun t => φ t - φ (γ * t)) (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ) = 0

The matching statement for Lab M2.2, that the transform of Σ f(nx) is ζ times the transform of f, is a natural next target; Mathlib has the Mellin transform and riemannZeta but not this identity in this form.
