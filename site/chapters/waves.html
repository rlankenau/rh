Zeros as waves

The explicit formula writes \(\psi(x)\) as \(x\) minus one oscillating term for each zero of \(\zeta\). A zero with real part \(\beta\) contributes a term of size about \(x^{\beta}\), so the real parts of the zeros determine the size of \(\psi(x) - x\).

The explicit formula

Riemann wrote down, and von Mangoldt later proved, an exact equation between the weighted staircase \(\psi(x)\) of chapter @@ch:primes@@ and the zeros of zeta in the critical strip. It is called the explicit formula. Each zero is a complex number, written \(\rho\):

$$\psi(x) \;=\; x \;-\; \sum_{\rho}\frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho} \;-\; \ln 2\pi \;-\; \tfrac12\ln\!\left(1-x^{-2}\right)$$

Read it left to right. The first term, \(x\), is the smooth guess. Then comes one term for every zero. The last two terms are tiny corrections. So everything interesting is in the terms for the zeros.

Write a zero as \(\rho = \beta + i\gamma\). Then \(x^{\rho} = x^{\beta} \cdot e^{i\gamma \ln x}\), and the zero together with its complex conjugate \(\beta - i\gamma\) contributes a real oscillation:

  • its frequency is \(\gamma\): the oscillation is \(\cos(\gamma \ln x)\), periodic on a logarithmic scale;
  • its amplitude is about \(2x^{\beta}/\gamma\), so it grows like \(x^{\beta}\) and higher zeros contribute less.

What a zero off the line would do

If every zero is on the critical line, \(\beta = \tfrac12\), then every wave has amplitude about \(\sqrt{x}\), and the total gap is about \(\sqrt{x}\): the bound from chapter @@ch:primes@@. A single zero with \(\beta\) bigger than \(\tfrac12\) would contribute a wave of size \(x^{\beta}\), which eventually drowns out all the others. This is why the Riemann Hypothesis and the square-root bound on the gap are the same statement.

The mirror symmetry of chapter @@ch:zeta-zeros@@ matters here. A zero to the left of the line has a partner the same distance to the right, so any zero off the line produces a wave that grows faster than \(\sqrt{x}\).

Exercises

  1. Show that \(x^{\rho} + x^{\bar{\rho}} = 2x^{\beta} \cos(\gamma \ln x)\) for \(\rho = \beta + i\gamma\).
    Solution

    \(x^{\rho} = x^{\beta} e^{i\gamma \ln x}\) and \(x^{\bar{\rho}} = x^{\beta} e^{-i\gamma \ln x}\). Their sum is \(x^{\beta} \cdot 2 \cos(\gamma \ln x)\), because \(e^{i\theta} + e^{-i\theta} = 2 \cos \theta\).

  2. By what factor must \(x\) grow for the wave of the first zero (\(\gamma \approx 14.13\)) to complete one full oscillation?
    Solution

    \(\cos(\gamma \ln x)\) repeats when \(\ln x\) increases by \(2\pi/\gamma \approx 0.445\), that is, when \(x\) is multiplied by \(e^{0.445} \approx 1.56\).

  3. Two zeros have the same height, one with \(\beta = 0.5\) and one with \(\beta = 0.6\). What is the ratio of their amplitudes at \(x = 10^{6}\)?
    Solution

    \(x^{0.6}/x^{0.5} = x^{0.1} = 10^{0.6} \approx 4\). The ratio grows without bound as \(x\) does.

Check your understanding

  1. Why is "all zeros on the line \(\sigma = \tfrac12\)" the best possible case for the size of the error?

    • Because \(\tfrac12\) is the smallest real part a zero can have
    • Because zeros come in mirror pairs \(\beta\) and \(1 - \beta\), so the largest real part is at least \(\tfrac12\), with equality only if all are on the line
    • Because waves with \(\beta = \tfrac12\) cancel each other

    The error is as large as its largest wave, \(x^{\max \beta}\). A zero with \(\beta \lt \tfrac12\) forces a partner with \(1 - \beta \gt \tfrac12\), so nothing can beat \(\sqrt{x}\).

  2. Each wave is \(\cos(\gamma \ln x)\). How do its wiggles look near \(x = 1{,}000{,}000\) compared with near \(x = 1{,}000\)?

    • The same width
    • Narrower
    • A thousand times wider

    The wave is periodic in \(\ln x\): one cycle is a fixed ratio of \(x\) (1.56 for the first zero). So the width in \(x\) is proportional to \(x\). This is why the labs use a logarithmic axis.

  3. What do the zeros at large height add to the reconstruction of the prime staircase?

    • Fine detail: the sharpness of each individual jump
    • The overall slope
    • Nothing; only the first few zeros matter

    A large \(\gamma\) is a high frequency. The first few zeros give the broad shape; resolving a single jump at a prime needs zeros with periods shorter than the gap between primes.
