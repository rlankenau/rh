The zeta function

The zeta function is the sum ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + … This chapter treats it for real numbers s only: when the sum has a value, and why it equals a product over the primes.

The sum

Fix a real number s and add up 1/ns over the whole numbers n = 1, 2, 3, … For s = 2 the terms are 1, 1/4, 1/9, 1/16, …, and the sum is 1.6449… (Euler showed it is exactly π²/6). The larger s is, the faster the terms shrink.

For s = 1 the terms are 1, 1/2, 1/3, 1/4, … They shrink, but too slowly: the sum of the first N terms is about ln N + 0.577, which grows without bound. This is the harmonic series, and it has no finite sum.

The dividing line is s = 1. To see why, compare each term with an area. The term 1/ns is the area of a rectangle of width 1 and height 1/ns. Place that rectangle on the interval from n − 1 to n. The curve y = 1/xs is decreasing, so on that interval it lies above the rectangle, and the area under the curve is larger:

$$\frac{1}{n^{s}} \;\le\; \int_{n-1}^{n} \frac{dx}{x^{s}}$$

Add this up over n = N + 1, N + 2, … The intervals join up into everything to the right of N:

$$\sum_{n>N} \frac{1}{n^{s}} \;\le\; \int_{N}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{x^{s}} \;=\; \frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1} \qquad \text{when } s>1$$

So for s > 1 the terms after the N-th add up to at most N1 − s/(s − 1), which shrinks to 0 as N grows. The sum converges.

Placing each rectangle one step to the right, on the interval from n to n + 1, puts it above the curve and gives the opposite inequality: the terms from the N-th on add up to at least the same integral. For s ≤ 1 that integral is infinite, so the sum is too. The two bounds differ by less than a single term, so the integral is also a good estimate of what the remaining terms add up to.

So ζ(s) is defined, for now, only for s > 1.

Near s = 1

As s decreases toward 1 the sum grows without bound. The integral above with N = 1 says how: ζ(s) is close to 1/(s − 1). More precisely, ζ(s) − 1/(s − 1) tends to 0.5772…, a number called Euler's constant.

The product over primes

Recall the geometric series: for |x| < 1, 1 + x + x² + x³ + … = 1/(1 − x). Put x = 1/2s:

$$\frac{1}{1-2^{-s}} \;=\; 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\frac{1}{8^{s}}+\cdots$$ This is the part of the zeta sum that runs over the powers of 2.

Do the same for 3 and multiply the two series together:

$$\frac{1}{1-2^{-s}}\cdot\frac{1}{1-3^{-s}} \;=\; \left(1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\cdots\right)\left(1+\frac{1}{3^{s}}+\frac{1}{9^{s}}+\cdots\right)$$ $$=\; 1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\frac{1}{3^{s}}+\frac{1}{4^{s}}+\frac{1}{6^{s}}+\frac{1}{8^{s}}+\frac{1}{9^{s}}+\frac{1}{12^{s}}+\cdots$$

Each product of a term from the first bracket and a term from the second is 1/ns for n = 2a3b, and each such n occurs once. The numbers 5, 7, 10, 11, … are missing because they have a prime factor other than 2 and 3. Include a factor for every prime, and every whole number appears exactly once, because every whole number is a product of primes in exactly one way. This is Euler's product formula:

$$\zeta(s) \;=\; \prod_{p\ \text{prime}} \frac{1}{1-p^{-s}} \qquad \text{for } s>1$$

Three consequences

  • ζ(s) is never 0 for s > 1. Every factor of the product is greater than 1.
  • There are infinitely many primes. If there were finitely many, the product would have a finite value at s = 1. But as s decreases to 1 the sum grows without bound.
  • The weights of chapter @@ch:primes@@ appear. Take the logarithm of the product, \(\displaystyle \ln\zeta(s) = -\sum_{p}\ln\!\left(1-p^{-s}\right)\), and differentiate:

$$-\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} \;=\; \sum_{n} \frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}$$ Here Λ(n) = ln p if n is a power of the prime p, and 0 otherwise.

The left side is built from ζ alone. The right side is a sum over prime powers with the same weights that define ψ(x). Exercise 2 derives it.

Exercises

  1. Multiply out the Euler product for s = 2 using only the primes 2 and 3, and compare with ζ(2) ≈ 1.645.
    Solution

    1/(1 − ¼) · 1/(1 − 1/9) = (4/3)(9/8) = 1.5. Multiplied out, this is the sum of 1/n² over the numbers n whose only prime factors are 2 and 3. The missing 0.145 comes from every n divisible by a larger prime.

  2. Derive \(\displaystyle -\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} = \sum_{n}\frac{\Lambda(n)}{n^{s}}\) from the Euler product.
    Solution

    Start from \(\displaystyle \ln\zeta(s) = -\sum_{p}\ln\!\left(1-p^{-s}\right)\) and differentiate: $$\frac{\zeta^{\prime}(s)}{\zeta(s)} \;=\; -\sum_{p} \ln p\cdot\frac{p^{-s}}{1-p^{-s}} \;=\; -\sum_{p} \ln p\cdot\left(p^{-s}+p^{-2s}+p^{-3s}+\cdots\right)$$ The second step is the geometric series. The term p−ks is 1/ns for n = pk, with coefficient ln p = Λ(n).

  3. Use the integral estimate to say how many terms are needed to get ζ(2) to within 0.001, and ζ(1.1) to within 0.001.
    Solution

    The missing tail is about N1 − s/(s − 1). For s = 2 that is 1/N, so N ≈ 1,000. For s = 1.1 it is 10 · N−0.1, which is below 0.001 only when N > 1040. Close to s = 1 the sum converges, but far too slowly to compute by adding terms.

Check your understanding

  1. The terms 1/n shrink to 0, yet the harmonic series has no finite sum. What decides whether Σ 1/ns converges?

    • Whether the terms tend to 0
    • How fast they shrink: the tail behaves like ∫ dx/xs, which is finite only for s > 1
    • Whether s is a whole number

    Terms tending to 0 is necessary but not enough. At s = 1 the first N terms add up to about ln N, which is unbounded.

  2. The product using only the primes 2 and 3 is, multiplied out, a sum of 1/ns over which n?

    • All n up to 3
    • The multiples of 2 or 3
    • The n with no prime factor other than 2 and 3: 1, 2, 3, 4, 6, 8, 9, 12, …

    Each term of the expanded product is 1/(2a3b)s. 10 is a multiple of 2 but is missing, because it also needs the prime 5.

  3. How does the product formula show there are infinitely many primes?

    • With finitely many primes the product would stay finite as s → 1, but the sum grows without bound there
    • The product has infinitely many factors by definition
    • It does not; that needs a separate argument

    Sum and product are equal for every s > 1. A finite product of the factors 1/(1 − p−s) tends to a finite number as s → 1, while the sum behaves like 1/(s − 1). This was Euler's proof.
