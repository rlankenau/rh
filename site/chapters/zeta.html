The zeta function

The zeta function is ζ(s) = Σ 1/ns. This chapter covers its product formula over the primes, its extension to complex s, and its zeros. The Riemann Hypothesis is a statement about where those zeros are.

A sum that is also a product

For real s > 1, the zeta function is the convergent series ζ(s) = 1 + 1/2s + 1/3s + 1/4s + … For example ζ(2) = 1 + 1/4 + 1/9 + 1/16 + … ≈ 1.645. Euler found that the same number is a product with one factor per prime:

ζ(s) = Πp prime 1 / (1 − p−s). Multiply the factors out and every whole number appears exactly once, because every whole number factors into primes in exactly one way.

The series runs over all whole numbers; the product runs over primes only. Take the logarithm of the product and differentiate, and the weights of chapter 1 appear: −ζ′(s)/ζ(s) = Σ Λ(n)/ns. The left side blows up wherever ζ(s) = 0. That is how the zeros of ζ enter the counting of primes.

Complex inputs and the critical strip

Riemann's step was to let s be a complex number, s = σ + it. Here σ is the real part and t is the imaginary part, which this site calls the height. The value ζ(s) is then complex too.

The series converges only for σ > 1. The function it defines there has a unique analytic continuation to the rest of the complex plane, except for a pole at s = 1. Three facts about the extended function organize the subject:

  • Right of 1 there are no zeros. There the product over primes is valid, and a product of nonzero factors is not zero.
  • A symmetry. A formula called the functional equation ties the value at s to the value at 1 − s. Its effect is that zeros come in mirror pairs on either side of the vertical line σ = ½.
  • What is left. Apart from some easy "trivial" zeros at −2, −4, −6…, every zero lies in the band between σ = 0 and σ = 1, called the critical strip. The line σ = ½ down its middle is the critical line.

The Riemann Hypothesis: every zero in the critical strip is on the critical line. Because zeros come in mirror pairs, this is the same as saying there are none to the right of the line, with σ > ½.

Watching a zero happen

A complex value is zero only when its real and imaginary parts are both zero. Plot ζ(σ + it) in the complex plane as t increases: the point loops around, and a zero is a loop that passes exactly through the origin.

How the lab computes ζ

The defining sum does not settle on a value inside the critical strip, so the lab uses a method due to Borwein that does, with 70 terms. Checked against published values it is accurate to about six decimal places for heights up to 60, which is why the labs stop there.
