Taylor series

A Taylor series rebuilds a function from its derivatives at a single point. This chapter recalls how the polynomials are formed, what each term contributes, and what it means for the series to converge.

Approximating a function by polynomials

Near a point \(c\), called the center, a function that has derivatives of every order can be approximated by polynomials built from its derivatives at \(c\):

$$T_N(x) \;=\; f(c) \;+\; f'(c)\,(x-c) \;+\; \frac{f''(c)}{2!}\,(x-c)^{2} \;+\; \cdots \;+\; \frac{f^{(N)}(c)}{N!}\,(x-c)^{N}$$

Each term fixes one more thing about the graph at the point \(c\):

  • \(T_0(x) = f(c)\) is a horizontal line. It has the right value at \(c\) and nothing else.
  • \(T_1(x) = f(c) + f'(c)\,(x-c)\) is the tangent line. It has the right value and the right slope.
  • \(T_2\) adds the term \(\dfrac{f''(c)}{2}(x-c)^{2}\) and becomes a parabola. It has the right value, slope and bending (second derivative).
  • \(T_3\) adds a cubic term that makes the third derivative right as well, and so on.

Adding a term never spoils what the earlier terms achieved, because \((x-c)^{k}\) and its first \(k-1\) derivatives are all 0 at \(c\). The factor \(1/k!\) is there because the \(k\)-th derivative of \((x-c)^{k}\) is \(k!\). So \(T_N\) is the one polynomial of degree \(N\) whose value and first \(N\) derivatives at \(c\) agree with those of \(f\).

The Taylor series of \(f\) about \(c\) is what you get by never stopping:

$$\sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{f^{(k)}(c)}{k!}\,(x-c)^{k}$$

Three series to know

All three are about the center \(c = 0\):

  • \(e^{x} = 1 + x + \dfrac{x^{2}}{2!} + \dfrac{x^{3}}{3!} + \cdots\). Every derivative of \(e^{x}\) is \(e^{x}\), which is 1 at 0. The series converges to \(e^{x}\) for every \(x\).
  • \(\sin x = x - \dfrac{x^{3}}{3!} + \dfrac{x^{5}}{5!} - \cdots\). The derivatives at 0 cycle through 0, 1, 0, −1. The series converges to \(\sin x\) for every \(x\).
  • \(\dfrac{1}{1-x} = 1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + \cdots\). The \(k\)-th derivative at 0 is \(k!\), so every coefficient is 1. The geometric series is the Taylor series of \(1/(1-x)\), and it converges only for \(|x| \lt 1\).

A Taylor series always converges on an interval that is symmetric about the center, \(|x - c| \lt R\), and diverges for \(|x - c| \gt R\). The number \(R\) is the radius of convergence. It can be infinite, as for \(e^{x}\), or finite, as for \(1/(1-x)\), where \(R = 1\).

Three things a Taylor series can do

  • Converge to the function everywhere. \(e^{x}\) and \(\sin x\).
  • Converge to the function on an interval and diverge outside it. \(1/(1-x)\), \(\ln(1+x)\) and \(1/(1+x^{2})\), each with \(R = 1\) about the center 0.
  • Converge, but to the wrong function. Let \(g(x) = 0\) for \(x \le 0\) and \(g(x) = e^{-1/x}\) for \(x \gt 0\). Every derivative of \(g\) at 0 equals 0, so its Taylor series about 0 is \(0 + 0\cdot x + 0\cdot x^{2} + \cdots\). That series converges for every \(x\), to 0, which is not \(g(x)\) when \(x \gt 0\). It is the last function in Lab @@n@@.2.

So "has a Taylor series" and "equals its Taylor series" are different properties. Chapter @@ch:analytic@@ is about the functions that have the second one.

Exercises

  1. Compute the Taylor polynomial \(T_3\) of \(\ln(1+x)\) about 0 and use it to estimate \(\ln 1.1\).
    Solution

    The derivatives of \(\ln(1+x)\) at 0 are 1, −1, 2, so $$T_3(x) \;=\; x - \frac{x^{2}}{2} + \frac{x^{3}}{3}$$ At \(x = 0.1\) this is \(0.1 - 0.005 + 0.000333 = 0.095333\). The true value is \(\ln 1.1 = 0.095310\).

  2. Find the Taylor polynomial \(T_2\) of \(\sqrt{x}\) about \(c = 4\) and use it to estimate \(\sqrt{4.1}\).
    Solution

    \(f(4) = 2\). \(f^{\prime}(x) = \dfrac{1}{2\sqrt{x}}\) gives \(f^{\prime}(4) = \tfrac14\), and \(f^{\prime\prime}(x) = -\dfrac{1}{4x^{3/2}}\) gives \(f^{\prime\prime}(4) = -\tfrac{1}{32}\). So $$T_2(x) \;=\; 2 + \frac{x-4}{4} - \frac{(x-4)^{2}}{64}$$ At \(x = 4.1\) this is \(2 + 0.025 - 0.000156 = 2.024844\). The true value is \(2.024846\).

  3. Why is the coefficient of \(x^{3}\) in the series for \(\sin x\) equal to \(-\tfrac16\)?
    Solution

    The third derivative of \(\sin x\) is \(-\cos x\), which is \(-1\) at 0. The coefficient is \(\dfrac{f^{\prime\prime\prime}(0)}{3!} = \dfrac{-1}{6}\).

Check your understanding

  1. \(T_1\) is the tangent line at \(c\). What does \(T_2\) add to it?

    • A better value at \(c\)
    • A term that makes the second derivative at \(c\) right, so the polynomial bends the way the graph does
    • A correction far from \(c\) only

    The term \(\tfrac{f^{\prime\prime}(c)}{2}(x-c)^{2}\) is 0 at \(c\) and has slope 0 there, so it leaves the value and the slope alone and fixes only the bending.

  2. In Lab @@n@@.2 the polynomial of degree 8 for \(1/(1+x^{2})\) fits well between −1 and 1 and badly outside. Will a higher degree fix the fit at \(x = 2\)?

    • Yes, eventually
    • Yes, but only for even degrees
    • No. \(x = 2\) is outside the radius of convergence, where adding terms makes the polynomial worse

    Inside \(|x| \lt R\) the polynomials converge to the function. Outside, the terms grow, and no degree helps.

  3. Every derivative of \(g\) at 0 is 0. What is its Taylor series about 0, and does it equal \(g\)?

    • The zero series. It converges everywhere but equals \(g\) only for \(x \le 0\)
    • It has no Taylor series
    • The zero series, and so \(g\) is 0 everywhere

    A Taylor series is built only from the derivatives at the center. It cannot know what the function does away from that point unless the function is analytic.
