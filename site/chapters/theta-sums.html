Summing over whole numbers

Chapter @@ch:circle-slots@@ summed a function over the powers of γ, and the factor 1 − γ−s appeared in front of its Mellin transform. Summing over the whole numbers instead puts ζ(s) in front. The resulting functions are the θ-functions of the paper.

Based on §1.1.4 of the paper (page 12), read in full. The lab works over the ordinary integers with the trivial character, which is the Riemann zeta case; the paper works over any number field.

θ-functions

The paper uses the construction of chapter @@ch:circle-slots@@ with a different sum, the one at the centre of Tate's thesis. Take a function f on (0, ∞) and sum its values at all whole-number multiples of x:

Θf(x) = √x · Σn ≥ 1 f(nx). The paper calls functions of this form θ-functions. The Mellin transform is M(Θf)(s) = ζ(s + ½) · M(f)(s + ½).

Proof: by the rescaling property the transform of f(nx) is n−s · M(f)(s), and Σ n−s = ζ(s). Multiplying by √x replaces s by s + ½. (The sum converges for Re s > ½; the identity extends to all s by analytic continuation when Θf decays at both ends.) The factor ζ here plays the role that 1 − γ−s played on the circle.

So the transform of every θ-function is zero at the zeros of ζ, whatever f is. Because of the shift by ½, a zero ρ of ζ appears at s = ρ − ½. If the Riemann Hypothesis holds these points are all on the imaginary axis, at s = ±14.13i, ±21.02i, ±25.01i, …

Things to try

  • In Lab @@n@@.1, move f around and change its width. The blue transform changes shape completely; the orange one keeps its zeros at the same heights.
  • Look for a setting where the blue curve itself dips close to zero between two dots. The orange curve dips there as well, but the ratio in the bottom panel does not: it depends only on ζ. (Very close to such a dip the ratio is a quotient of two tiny numbers and gets noisy.)

Exercises

  1. Show that the Mellin transform of f(nx) is n−s · M(f)(s).
    Solution

    Substitute u = nx: ∫ f(nx) xs dx/x = ∫ f(u) (u/n)s du/u = n−s · M(f)(s). The measure dx/x is unchanged by rescaling.

  2. The first zero of ζ is ρ ≈ ½ + 14.13i. Where is the transform of Θf zero because of it?
    Solution

    At s = ρ − ½ ≈ 14.13i, since M(Θf)(s) contains the factor ζ(s + ½). That is the first dot in the lab.

  3. Why does Θf(x) blow up as x → 0 unless ∫ f dx = 0?
    Solution

    For small x the points nx are closely spaced, and x · Σ f(nx) is a Riemann sum for ∫ f. So Σ f(nx) ≈ (1/x) ∫ f, and Θf(x) ≈ x−½ ∫ f, which is unbounded unless the integral is 0.
