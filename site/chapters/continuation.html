Analytic continuation

A formula can stop working at a point where the function it describes is perfectly well defined. Analytic continuation is the fact that an analytic function given on a small region extends to a larger one in at most one way. This chapter shows what that means for one example: the function \(1/(1-x)\) and its geometric series \(1 + x + x^{2} + \cdots\). Chapter @@ch:continuing-zeta@@ does it for \(\zeta\).

A function and a formula are different things

The geometric series \(1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + \cdots\) converges for \(-1 \lt x \lt 1\), and there its sum is \(1/(1-x)\). Outside that interval the series has no sum: at \(x = 2\) its terms are 1, 2, 4, 8, …

The expression \(1/(1-x)\) has no such restriction. It is defined for every \(x\) except 1: at \(x = 2\) it is −1, at \(x = -3\) it is \(\tfrac14\).

So there is one function, \(1/(1-x)\), and the series is a way of computing it that works only on part of its domain. The series failing at \(x = 2\) says something about the series, not about the function.

Why the extension is forced

Suppose all you knew was the geometric series, valid on \((-1, 1)\). Could some other function agree with it there and differ from \(1/(1-x)\) elsewhere?

Not an analytic one. By the rigidity of chapter @@ch:analytic@@, two analytic functions that agree on an interval agree everywhere that both are defined. So a function given on a small region has at most one analytic extension to a larger one.

That extension, when it exists, is the analytic continuation. (Rigidity says there is at most one. It does not say there is one. The section "When there is no continuation" below gives an example with none.) It is not a convention or a choice of how to assign a value to a divergent sum. It is the same function, seen on more of its domain.

Extending step by step

The Taylor series of \(1/(1-x)\) about another center \(c\) is

$$\frac{1}{1-x} \;=\; \sum_{k\ge 0} \frac{(x-c)^{k}}{(1-c)^{k+1}}$$ This is valid when \(|x - c| \lt |1 - c|\).

The series converges out to the distance from the center to the bad point \(x = 1\), and no further. Starting from the original series (center 0, valid on \((-1, 1)\)), compute the function and its derivatives at \(c = -0.9\). The series about that center is valid on \((-2.8, 1)\), which reaches points the original did not. Repeating this extends the function as far to the left as desired.

On the real line this process cannot get past \(x = 1\). In the complex plane it can: a chain of overlapping discs can go around the bad point, and the function on the far side is still determined.

When there is no continuation

Rigidity answers "how many continuations?" with "at most one". Whether there is one at all is a separate question, and the answer depends on the bad points.

The re-centering process of the last section is stopped only by bad points. The series for \(1/(1-x)\) has a single bad point, at 1. A disc centered at \(c = -0.5\) has radius 1.5 and reaches out to \(-2\), well beyond the original interval. In the complex plane the discs can also pass around the point 1. So the continuation exists everywhere except at that one point.

Now take a different series:

$$f(x) \;=\; x + x^{2} + x^{4} + x^{8} + x^{16} + \cdots$$ The exponents are the powers of 2. Like the geometric series, it converges for \(|x| \lt 1\) and defines an analytic function there.

Where are its bad points? Allow complex inputs \(z\), as in chapter @@ch:analytic@@.

  • At \(z = 1\) every term equals 1, and the sum grows without bound as \(z\) approaches 1.
  • At \(z = -1\) every term after the first equals 1, since the exponents are even. Again the sum grows without bound.
  • At \(z = i\) every term after the second equals 1, since \(i^{4} = 1\). The same happens.
  • In general: if \(z^{2^{m}} = 1\) for some \(m\), then every term from the \(m\)-th on equals 1 at \(z\), and \(z\) is a bad point.

The equation \(z^{2^{m}} = 1\) has \(2^{m}\) solutions, equally spaced around the circle \(|z| = 1\). So there are 2 bad points, and 4, and 8, and 16, and so on without end. Every arc of the circle, however short, contains one.

For this function the circle \(|z| = 1\) is a wall of bad points. A disc of convergence cannot contain a bad point, so the Taylor series about any center \(c\) inside the circle has radius exactly \(1 - |c|\): it reaches the circle and stops. No chain of discs gets out. This function has no analytic continuation beyond the circle. The circle is called its natural boundary.

How would we know?

  • That a continuation exists: by producing it. Either re-center and check that the new disc reaches past the old boundary, or find a second formula that is analytic on a larger region and agrees with the first where both apply. For \(1/(1-x)\) the second formula is the fraction itself. For \(\zeta\), chapter @@ch:continuing-zeta@@ builds such a formula.
  • That none exists: by showing that the bad points block every route, as above.
  • Numerically: compute the function on circles approaching the boundary, as in the lab. Growth at isolated places suggests isolated bad points that can be passed. Growth everywhere suggests a wall. This is evidence, not proof.

There is one more way an extension can fail to be a single function. For \(\ln x\), continuing around the bad point 0 in the two possible directions gives two different answers on the far side. For \(\zeta\) this does not happen: its only bad point is \(s = 1\), and going around it either way gives the same values.

Exercises

  1. Derive the Taylor series of \(1/(1-x)\) about the center \(c\).
    Solution

    Write \(1 - x = (1-c) - (x-c) = (1-c)(1-u)\) with \(u = \dfrac{x-c}{1-c}\). Then $$\frac{1}{1-x} \;=\; \frac{1}{1-c}\left(1+u+u^{2}+\cdots\right)$$ valid for \(|u| \lt 1\), that is, \(|x - c| \lt |1 - c|\).

  2. In what sense is "\(1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots = -1\)" true, and in what sense false?
    Solution

    As a statement about adding numbers it is false: the partial sums 1, 3, 7, 15, … grow without bound. It is true as shorthand for "the function \(1/(1-x)\), whose series is \(1 + x + x^{2} + \cdots\) where that converges, has the value −1 at \(x = 2\)". The statement \(\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}\) is of the same kind.

  3. The Taylor series of \(1/(1-x)\) about \(c = -3\) converges on which interval? Does it give the value of the function at \(x = -6\)? At \(x = 2\)?
    Solution

    The radius is the distance from \(-3\) to the bad point 1, which is 4. The series converges for \(-7 \lt x \lt 1\). So it gives the value at \(x = -6\) (namely \(\tfrac17\)), but not at \(x = 2\). No center on the real line to the left of 1 gives a series that reaches \(x = 2\).

Check your understanding

  1. The geometric series diverges at \(x = 2\), and \(1/(1-x)\) equals −1 there. Which statement is right?

    • The series secretly adds up to −1
    • The function has the value −1; the series is a way of computing the function that works only for \(|x| \lt 1\)
    • The function is undefined at 2

    The sum \(1 + 2 + 4 + \cdots\) has no value. The function it came from does, and analytic continuation is about the function.

  2. Why is there only one analytic continuation?

    • Two analytic functions that agree on any interval agree wherever both are defined
    • Mathematicians agreed on a convention
    • Because the series converges everywhere

    Rigidity: the values near one point fix every Taylor coefficient, and so the whole function. This is what makes it legitimate to speak of \(\zeta(s)\) where the sum diverges.

  3. In Lab @@n@@.2, moving the center from 0 to \(-0.9\) changes the interval of convergence from \((-1, 1)\) to \((-2.8, 1)\). Why does the right end stay at 1?

    • Because the series was defined with 1 as its limit
    • Because \(x = 1\) is the bad point, and the series about any center converges only up to the nearest bad point
    • Because the degree is too low

    The radius is the distance from the center to the bad point. Moving the center left makes the radius larger, so the left end moves, but the right end is always the bad point itself.
