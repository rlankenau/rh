The smoothed error

The Alpöge formalization does not bound \(\psi(x) - x\). It bounds Gz\((x)\), the error term of a weighted integral of \(\psi\). Integrating divides the term for each zero by one more factor of size \(|\rho|\), and that makes the sum over zeros converge absolutely.

Based on the definitions of Sz, Gz and ZetaLandau in the Lean source, which I read as statements. The curves are computed here from those definitions and from the first 30 zeros; the match between the two is a check I ran, not part of the formalization.

The definition

def Sz (x : ℝ) : ℝ := ∑ n ∈ Finset.Icc 1 ⌊x⌋₊, Λ n * (x / n - 1)
def Gz (x : ℝ) : ℝ := Sz x - x * Real.log x + (1 + Real.eulerMascheroniConstant) * x

Sz weights each \(n \le x\) by \(\Lambda(n)\cdot(x/n - 1)\) instead of \(\Lambda(n)\). Equivalently, Sz\(\displaystyle (x) = x \cdot \int_{1}^{x} \frac{\psi(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt\). The weight is 0 at \(n = x\), so Sz is continuous: it has corners at the prime powers but no jumps. Its main term is \(x \ln x - (1 + \gamma_{\text{Euler}})x\), and Gz is Sz minus that main term.

What the zeros do to it

Apply the same integral to the explicit formula of chapter @@ch:waves@@, term by term. The term \(x^{\rho}/\rho\) becomes \(\dfrac{x^{\rho}}{\rho(\rho-1)}\):

$$\mathrm{Gz}(x) \;=\; \ln 2\pi \;-\; \sum_{\rho} \frac{x^{\rho}}{\rho\,(\rho-1)} \;-\; (\text{tiny terms})$$ If every zero is on the line this is $$\ln 2\pi \;+\; \sqrt{x}\,\sum_{\gamma>0} \frac{2\cos(\gamma \ln x)}{\tfrac14+\gamma^{2}}$$

The term for the zero at height \(\gamma\) now has amplitude about \(2/\gamma^{2}\) in units of \(\sqrt{x}\), where in chapter @@ch:waves@@ it was \(2/\gamma\). The sum of \(2/(\tfrac14 + \gamma^{2})\) over all zeros converges, to \(2 + \gamma_{\text{Euler}} - \ln 4\pi \approx 0.0462\). So if the Riemann Hypothesis holds, \(\lvert \mathrm{Gz}(x) - \ln 2\pi \rvert \le 0.0462 \cdot \sqrt{x}\) up to the tiny terms, and the first few zeros account for most of the sum.

Why one side is enough

The formalization proves an upper bound on Gz and no lower bound. A theorem of Landau makes that sufficient. The Lean statement ZetaLandau covers either sign: if \(s_{0}\cdot \mathrm{Gz}(x) \le C\cdot x^{\theta}\) for all large \(x\), with \(s_{0} = +1\) or \(-1\), then \(\zeta\) has no zeros with real part greater than \(\theta\).

A zero with real part \(\beta \gt \theta\) contributes an oscillating term of amplitude proportional to \(x^{\beta}\), which takes both signs. On its own that term exceeds \(C\cdot x^{\theta}\) in both directions for large \(x\). Landau's theorem shows that the terms from the other zeros cannot cancel it.

  • In Lab @@n@@.2, set \(\beta = 0.55\). The curve stays inside the band all the way to 20,000: the extra factor \(x^{0.05}\) is only 1.6 at \(x = 20{,}000\). Then try 0.7.
  • Raise \(\gamma\) with \(\beta\) fixed. The amplitude falls like \(1/\gamma^{2}\), so the curve leaves the band later. An off-line zero at very large height would not be visible in any range of \(x\) a computer can reach.

Where this sits in the proof

The formalization bounds a combined error Gm: a sum of versions of Gz twisted by Dirichlet characters, so that all Dirichlet L-functions are covered at once. It needs the bound only on a sparse sequence of values of \(x\). The map of the whole proof has the statements.

Exercises

  1. Compute Sz(4).
    Solution

    The prime powers up to 4 are 2, 3, 4. $$\mathrm{Sz}(4) \;=\; \ln 2\cdot\left(\frac{4}{2}-1\right) + \ln 3\cdot\left(\frac{4}{3}-1\right) + \ln 2\cdot\left(\frac{4}{4}-1\right) \;=\; \ln 2 + \frac{\ln 3}{3} + 0 \;\approx\; 1.059$$ The prime power 4 itself contributes nothing yet.

  2. Verify that \(\displaystyle \mathrm{Sz}(x) = x\int_{1}^{x}\frac{\psi(t)}{t^{2}}\,dt\).
    Solution

    \(\psi(t)\) is the sum of \(\Lambda(n)\) over \(n \le t\), so each \(n \le x\) contributes $$\Lambda(n)\int_{n}^{x}\frac{dt}{t^{2}} \;=\; \Lambda(n)\left(\frac{1}{n}-\frac{1}{x}\right)$$ Multiplying by \(x\) gives \(\Lambda(n)\cdot(x/n - 1)\).

  3. Apply the same integral to a single term \(t^{\rho}/\rho\) of the explicit formula.
    Solution

    $$x\int_{1}^{x}\frac{t^{\,\rho-2}}{\rho}\,dt \;=\; x\cdot\frac{x^{\,\rho-1}-1}{\rho\,(\rho-1)} \;=\; \frac{x^{\rho}-x}{\rho\,(\rho-1)}$$ The \(x^{\rho}\) part is the oscillating term; the parts proportional to \(x\) are absorbed into the main term.

Check your understanding

  1. What is gained by bounding the smoothed error Gz and not \(\psi(x) - x\) directly?

    • Gz has no contribution from zeros
    • The amplitudes become about \(2/\gamma^{2}\), whose sum over all zeros is finite, so the sum over zeros converges absolutely
    • Gz is easier to compute by hand

    For \(\psi\) the amplitudes are about \(2/\gamma\), and their sum diverges; the explicit formula then converges only through cancellation. The smoothed version can be bounded term by term.

  2. Why is Sz continuous although \(\psi\) jumps at every prime power?

    • Because the weight \(x/n - 1\) is 0 at the moment \(n\) enters the sum
    • Because primes are ignored
    • Because it is rounded

    A new term appears at \(x = n\) with weight 0 and then grows linearly. That gives a corner, not a jump. Integrating a function with jumps always does this.

  3. In Lab @@n@@.2 a hypothetical zero with \(\beta = 0.55\) stays inside the band up to 20,000. What does that say about testing the hypothesis by computing with primes?

    • It confirms the hypothesis
    • That such a zero is impossible
    • A zero close to the line would be invisible in any range of \(x\) one can compute, so such tests cannot settle it

    The excess factor is \(x^{0.05}\), only 1.6 at 20,000, and a zero at great height has a tiny amplitude as well. Evidence from primes in a finite range is not a proof.
