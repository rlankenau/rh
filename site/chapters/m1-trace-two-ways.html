A trace computed two ways

Masullo's proof is a trace formula: one number computed twice, once as a sum over zeros and once as a sum over primes. The introduction of the paper explains the mechanism on a toy example small enough to compute by hand. This chapter is that toy, with knobs.

Based on the toy example in the paper's introduction (pages 11–33), which I read in full. The lab computes both sides numerically; the Lean file RH/Toy.lean states the identity and proves one consequence from it.

A circle made by multiplying

Take the positive real numbers and pick a number γ > 1. Declare t and γt to be the same point, so 1, γ, γ², … all coincide. On a logarithmic ruler this rolls the line up into a circle of circumference ln γ.

Now pick a smooth bump φ on the positive reals and use it to smear functions on the circle: replace the value at each point by a φ-weighted average of nearby values. That smearing is a linear operator, and it has a trace: the sum of its eigenvalues. There are two ways to compute it.

  • By frequencies (the spectral side). The pure tones on the circle are the waves that fit a whole number m of times around. Smearing multiplies tone m by a number, the Mellin transform of φ at 2πim/ln γ. The trace is the sum of those numbers over all m.
  • By wrapping (the geometric side). The smear connects a point to itself once for every way of going around the circle k times, with strength φ(γk). The trace is the circumference times the sum of those strengths.

Σm M(φ)(2πim / ln γ) = ln γ · Σk φ(γk). This is Poisson summation, written multiplicatively.

The dictionary to the real thing

In the actual proof the circle is replaced by a much larger space built from all the completions of a number field at once, but the two sides keep their character:

  • the frequencies become the zeros of the L-function, one term for each;
  • the wraps become prime powers, each prime p contributing its own circle of circumference ln p, with terms ln p · φ(pk).

Read that way, a trace formula is the explicit formula of chapter 3: a sum over zeros equal to a sum over primes, for any test bump φ you like.

Making a trace vanish

One feature of the toy carries a lot of weight in the paper. If the bump sits strictly between 1 and γ, no power of γ lands inside it. The geometric side is then zero, so the sum over frequencies must be zero too, although the individual terms are not. In the paper this is how all but finitely many primes are made to drop out: for a fixed bump, every prime larger than the bump's reach contributes nothing.

In Lean

The repository states the identity and proves the vanishing from it. The identity itself is still a sorry; Mathlib has Poisson summation, and deriving this form from it is the next Lean task.

theorem trace_identity {γ : ℝ} (hγ : 1 < γ) {φ : ℝ → ℂ}
    (hφ : ContDiff ℝ ∞ φ) (hc : HasCompactSupport φ) (h0 : tsupport φ ⊆ Ioi 0) :
    ∑' m : ℤ, mellin φ (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ)
      = Real.log γ * ∑' k : ℤ, φ (γ ^ k) := by sorry

theorem trace_eq_zero … (hs : tsupport φ ⊆ Ioo 1 γ) :
    ∑' m : ℤ, mellin φ (2 * π * Complex.I * m / Real.log γ) = 0

Things to try

  • Move the bump centre until the bump fits between the dashed lines at 1 and γ. Both sides drop to zero. Then lower M to 1 or 2: the partial sums are not zero, only the full sum is.
  • Make the bump wide enough to cover two powers of γ. The geometric side counts both.
  • Shrink γ toward 1.3. More powers crowd under the bump and the trace grows: a small circle is wrapped more often.
