Continuing zeta

The sum for \(\zeta(s)\) has a value only when the real part of \(s\) is greater than 1. This chapter extends \(\zeta\) to every complex number except \(s = 1\): first to the half-plane \(\sigma \gt 0\) in seven small steps, then to the rest.

Chapter @@ch:continuation@@ extended \(1/(1-x)\) beyond the interval where its series converges. The same thing can be done for \(\zeta\). The steps start with real \(s\).

From \(\sigma \gt 1\) to \(\sigma \gt 0\), in seven steps

Step 1. What chapter @@ch:zeta@@ said about the terms left over

For \(s \gt 1\), chapter @@ch:zeta@@ compared the terms of the sum with rectangles under the curve \(y = 1/x^{s}\). The conclusion was that the terms after the \(N\)-th add up to about

$$\int_{N}^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x^{s}} \;=\; \frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1}$$

So the whole sum is close to the first \(N\) terms plus that amount:

$$\zeta(s) \;\approx\; \underbrace{1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{N^{s}}}_{\text{the first } N \text{ terms}} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{N^{1-s}}{s-1}}_{\text{estimate of the rest}}$$

Check it at \(s = 2\), where \(\zeta(2) = 1.6449\) and the estimate of the rest is \(1/N\):

The first \(N\) terms alone approach 1.6449 slowly. With the estimate added, the total is already right to two decimal places at \(N = 10\), and the approximation becomes exact as \(N \to \infty\).

Step 2. Write the same thing so that it can be used for other \(s\)

Since \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{s-1} = -\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}\), step 1 says:

$$\zeta(s) \;=\; \lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\;1+\frac{1}{2^{s}}+\dots+\frac{1}{N^{s}} \;-\; \frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}\;\right]$$

For \(s \gt 1\) this is nothing new: the subtracted term tends to 0, and what is left is the original sum.

Step 3. Notice that the bracket makes sense for every \(s \ne 1\)

Look at the two pieces inside the bracket for a fixed \(N\).

  • \(1 + \dfrac{1}{2^{s}} + \dots + \dfrac{1}{N^{s}}\) is a sum of \(N\) numbers. It has a value for every \(s\).
  • \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}\) has a value for every \(s\) except \(s = 1\).

So the bracket can be computed for any \(s \ne 1\) and any \(N\), including values of \(s\) where the infinite sum diverges. The only question is whether it settles down as \(N\) grows.

Step 4. Try it where the sum diverges: \(s = \tfrac12\)

At \(s = \tfrac12\) the terms are \(1/\sqrt{n}\), and the sum grows without bound. The subtracted term is \(\dfrac{N^{1/2}}{1/2} = 2\sqrt{N}\), which also grows without bound.

Step 5. Why the difference settles

The subtracted term is an area under the curve: \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s} = \dfrac{1}{1-s} + \displaystyle\int_{1}^{N}\frac{dx}{x^{s}}\). Split that area into the pieces over \([1,2], [2,3], \dots\) and pair each piece with one term of the sum:

$$\text{bracket} \;=\; 1 \;-\; \frac{1}{1-s} \;+\; \sum_{n=2}^{N}\left(\frac{1}{n^{s}} - \int_{n-1}^{n}\frac{dx}{x^{s}}\right)$$

Each term in the new sum is the difference between a rectangle and the area under the curve above it, as in the picture of Lab @@ch:zeta@@.1. The curve gets flatter as \(n\) grows, so that difference is small: about \(\dfrac{s}{2\,n^{s+1}}\) in size. The numbers \(1/n^{s+1}\) have a finite sum whenever \(s \gt 0\). So the bracket converges for every \(s \gt 0\) except \(s = 1\).

Step 6. The same formula for complex \(s\)

Nothing in steps 2 to 5 needs \(s\) to be real. For complex \(s = \sigma + it\) the bracket converges whenever \(\sigma \gt 0\) and \(s \ne 1\).

In the picture of chapter @@ch:complex-zeta@@: for \(0 \lt \sigma \lt 1\) the chain of vectors does not settle. It circles in an ever wider spiral, and the term \(N^{1-s}/(1-s)\) is exactly the vector from the center of that spiral to the chain's current end. Subtracting it leaves the center, and the center is \(\zeta(s)\).

Step 7. Why this is the continuation

  • The limit of the bracket is a function defined for every \(s\) with \(\sigma \gt 0\) except \(s = 1\).
  • For \(\sigma \gt 1\) it equals the original sum (step 2).
  • It is analytic. (This needs a proof, which uses the fact that the convergence in step 5 is uniform.)

By the rigidity of chapter @@ch:analytic@@, there is only one analytic function with these properties. So the limit of the bracket is the continuation of \(\zeta\) to the half-plane \(\sigma \gt 0\).

From \(\sigma \gt 0\) to the whole plane

So far \(\zeta(s)\) is defined for every \(s\) with real part \(\sigma \gt 0\), except \(s = 1\). This section extends it to the remaining half of the plane, in two ways, and then looks at two famous values.

First way: subtract more of what grows

In step 5 the bracket converged because subtracting \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}\) removed the part of the sum that grows fastest. For \(\sigma \le 0\) that is no longer enough: a smaller growing part is left. It can be removed too. The pattern continues, and the first three corrections are:

$$\zeta(s) \;=\; \lim_{N\to\infty}\left[\;\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac{1}{n^{s}} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{N^{1-s}}{1-s}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt 0} \;-\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{2}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{s}}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt -1} \;+\; \underbrace{\frac{s}{12}\cdot\frac{1}{N^{s+1}}}_{\text{enough for } \sigma \gt -3}\;\right]$$

The second correction is half of the last term. The third comes from the next term of the same expansion (the Euler–Maclaurin formula, which refines the comparison between a sum and an integral). Each further correction moves the boundary further left, and with enough of them any \(s\) is reached. By rigidity, every one of these formulas gives the same function wherever two of them both apply.

Worked example: \(s = 0\)

At \(s = 0\) every term \(1/n^{0}\) equals 1, so the sum of the first \(N\) terms is \(N\). The original sum would be \(1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots\), which has no value. Now apply the corrections:

  • The first correction is \(\dfrac{N^{1}}{1} = N\). Subtracting it leaves \(N - N = 0\).
  • The second correction is half of the last term, \(\tfrac12\). Subtracting it leaves \(-\tfrac12\).
  • The third correction is \(\dfrac{0}{12}\cdot\dfrac1N = 0\).

The bracket equals \(-\tfrac12\) for every \(N\). So \(\zeta(0) = -\tfrac12\).

Worked example: \(s = -1\)

At \(s = -1\) the terms are \(1/n^{-1} = n\), so the sum of the first \(N\) terms is \(1 + 2 + \cdots + N\). There is an exact formula for that:

$$1 + 2 + \cdots + N \;=\; \frac{N(N+1)}{2} \;=\; \frac{N^{2}}{2} + \frac{N}{2}$$

Now the corrections, with \(s = -1\):

  • The first is \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s} = \dfrac{N^{2}}{2}\). It is exactly the first growing part of the sum.
  • The second is half of the last term, \(\dfrac{N}{2}\). It is exactly the second growing part.
  • The third is \(\dfrac{s}{12}\cdot\dfrac{1}{N^{s+1}} = \dfrac{-1}{12}\cdot\dfrac{1}{N^{0}} = -\dfrac{1}{12}\).

The sum \(1 + 2 + \cdots + N\) grows without bound, and all of its growth is in the two pieces \(N^{2}/2\) and \(N/2\). The continuation removes exactly those pieces. What remains does not depend on \(N\): it is \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\). So \(\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}\).

The statement "\(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots = -\tfrac{1}{12}\)" is shorthand for \(\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}\). It does not say that adding the whole numbers gives a negative fraction: that sum has no value. It says that the function which equals \(\sum 1/n^{s}\) where the sum converges has the value \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\) at \(s = -1\). In the same way, "\(1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots = -1\)" is shorthand for the value of \(1/(1-x)\) at \(x = 2\).

All real \(s\) at once

The two examples are single points on a curve. The next lab draws \(\zeta(s)\) for real \(s\) from 3 down to \(-2.2\), using as many corrections as each \(s\) needs.

Second way: a chain of discs around the pole

The same extension can be reached with Taylor series alone, as for \(1/(1-x)\) in Lab @@ch:continuation@@.2. The only bad point of \(\zeta\) is \(s = 1\). So by the rule of chapter @@ch:analytic@@, the Taylor series of \(\zeta\) about any center \(c\) converges in the disc around \(c\) whose edge passes through 1.

Start at a center where the original sum converges, for example \(c = 2\). Its disc reaches from 1 to 3. Pick a new center inside that disc, a little above the real axis. The function and all its derivatives are known there, so the Taylor series about the new center is known, and its disc covers points the first disc did not. Repeat. The chain of centers passes over the bad point and comes down on the other side.

What Riemann did

Riemann did not use either of these step-by-step methods. He found formulas for \(\zeta(s)\) that are valid for every \(s \ne 1\) at once. One of them, the functional equation, is in chapter @@ch:zeta-zeros@@. By rigidity all of these routes give the same function.

From here on, \(\zeta(s)\) means the extended function, defined for every complex \(s\) except \(s = 1\). At \(s = 1\) it has a pole: it behaves like \(1/(s-1)\), as in Lab @@ch:zeta@@.2.

Exercises

  1. Use the formula for \(\zeta\) with \(N = 1\), and half of the last term removed, to estimate \(\zeta(\tfrac12)\).
    Solution

    \(1 - \tfrac12 - \dfrac{1^{1/2}}{1 - \tfrac12} = 1 - \tfrac12 - 2 = -1.5\). The true value is −1.4604. With \(N = 10\) the same recipe gives −1.4617.

  2. Compute the bracket with all three corrections for \(s = 0\) and \(N = 5\).
    Solution

    The first 5 terms are each 1, so their sum is 5. The corrections are \(\dfrac{5^{1}}{1} = 5\), half of the last term \(= \tfrac12\), and \(\dfrac{0}{12}\cdot\dfrac15 = 0\). The bracket is \(5 - 5 - \tfrac12 + 0 = -\tfrac12\), the same as for every other \(N\).

  3. Use \(1^{2} + 2^{2} + \cdots + N^{2} = \dfrac{N^{3}}{3} + \dfrac{N^{2}}{2} + \dfrac{N}{6}\) to find \(\zeta(-2)\).
    Solution

    At \(s = -2\) the terms are \(n^{2}\). The corrections are \(\dfrac{N^{1-s}}{1-s} = \dfrac{N^{3}}{3}\), half of the last term \(= \dfrac{N^{2}}{2}\), and \(\dfrac{s}{12}\cdot\dfrac{1}{N^{s+1}} = -\dfrac{2}{12}N = -\dfrac{N}{6}\), which is added. So the bracket is \(\dfrac{N^{3}}{3} + \dfrac{N^{2}}{2} + \dfrac{N}{6} - \dfrac{N^{3}}{3} - \dfrac{N^{2}}{2} - \dfrac{N}{6} = 0\) for every \(N\). So \(\zeta(-2) = 0\). Lab @@n@@.11 shows the curve crossing 0 there.

Check your understanding

  1. In Lab @@n@@.6 with \(\sigma = 0.5\) the chain of vectors never settles. What is \(\zeta(s)\) in that picture?

    • The point where the chain ends
    • It does not exist
    • The center that the chain spirals around

    The chain's end is the center plus the vector \(N^{1-s}/(1-s)\), whose length grows like \(N^{1-\sigma}\). Removing that vector leaves the center, which is the continued function.

  2. At \(s = \tfrac12\) the sum of the first \(N\) terms and the term \(2\sqrt{N}\) both grow without bound. Why does their difference have a limit?

    • Because both are infinite, and infinity minus infinity is 0
    • Because the sum eventually stops growing
    • Because \(2\sqrt{N}\) is, up to a constant, the area under the curve that the sum approximates, and the leftover rectangle-minus-area pieces have a finite total

    Each term of the sum is paired with the area under the curve above it. The differences shrink like \(1/n^{s+1}\), fast enough to add up to a finite number.

  3. What does \(\zeta(-1) = -\tfrac{1}{12}\) say about \(1 + 2 + 3 + \cdots\)?

    • Nothing about adding the numbers up: that sum has no value. It gives the value at \(s = -1\) of the function that the sum defines where it converges
    • That the partial sums approach \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\)
    • That the sum equals \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\) if enough terms are taken

    The partial sums are \(N^{2}/2 + N/2\) and grow without bound. The continuation removes exactly those growing pieces; \(-\tfrac{1}{12}\) is what is left.
